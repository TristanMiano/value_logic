# R6.17 · What an agent can come to want under permanent pressure

Without assuming a goal is clamped, I would call a priority **terminal in practice** over the circumstances in which an agent keeps paying a price to act on it. The **whole value function** can encode the agent’s choices when its inputs distinguish the available actions and its rule for reading the scores is specified. Different scores or rules can give the same choices. Its **maxima** can identify the states offering the best long-run prospects under given opportunities and pressures. Changing the opportunities can move those peaks. A **summary of behavior**, such as “seeks safety”, is meaningful over the menus, costs and times observed and can change when tested on new ones. Under a lasting need, a revisable priority can remain common indefinitely and guide costly choices across different final ends. That resembles an ultimate want in what the agent does and what it sacrifices. To establish that it values safety for its own sake, one would also want choices that distinguish safety from the other benefits it brings.

## 1. How much will an agent give up for a useful intermediate state?

A test of **terminality in practice** specifies a choice, a price, the changes in ends or circumstances, and the duration. A deterministic controller passes if it strictly prefers the costly action throughout those tests. For a sampling controller, measure its choice odds against a declared reference. The price it pays and the range of changes it withstands are two degrees of entrenchment. We first derive the price for histories retained by an external filter, then test a deployed controller.

Imagine an agent who must stay alive to reach a destination. Each step it survives in state $`i`$ with probability $`s_i>0`$, then moves according to a baseline transition matrix $`P`$. Let $`K_{ij}=s_iP_{ij}`$, with finitely many states and every entry positive. At the assessment, destination $`j`$ has acceptance weight $`u_j`$. Allow any nonnegative weights other than the all-zero vector. Scaling all weights by one positive constant preserves comparisons. A failed trial has acceptance weight zero. The end can favor just one live state.

**1. A sharp price for agreement across ends.** At a fixed decision, let $`x`$ and $`y`$ be the live successor probability rows under two actions, including their respective chances of surviving this step. Each has positive total mass. After the action, use the same baseline for $`m\ge1`$ more steps. Impose a one-time price on the first action by multiplying its live-success weight by $`1/d`$, with $`d\ge1`$. For example, $`d=6/5`$ adds a one-in-six chance of losing the trial. The first action beats the second for **every** final assessment precisely when

```math
d < L_m,\qquad
L_m=\min_j\frac{(xK^m)_j}{(yK^m)_j}.
```

The lowest relative success factor over all ends is $`L_m`$. The highest, $`U_m`$, uses the maximum column ratio. These intervals nest as the horizon grows. They shrink to the single number $`xh/(yh)`$, where $`Kh=\lambda h`$ and $`h>0`$ is the leading right eigenvector. Thus whenever $`xh>yh`$, a whole interval of positive prices is eventually worth paying, uniformly over the agents’ final ends.

*Proof.* Write $`a=xK^m`$ and $`b=yK^m`$. For an end $`u`$, the two success weights are $`au`$ and $`bu`$. Their ratio is an average of $`a_j/b_j`$ with weights $`b_ju_j/(bu)`$. An assessment accepting only $`j`$ attains its ratio. Multiplying both rows by $`K`$ replaces each column ratio by another weighted average of the old ones, proving nesting. The positive-matrix limit $`\lambda^{-m}K^m\to hv^\top/(v^\top h)`$ with $`v>0`$ gives the same limit for every column. There are finitely many columns, so the extremes converge too. For an agent that samples both actions with positive baseline probabilities, Bayes’ rule multiplies their original odds among accepted trials by $`(au)/(d\,bu)`$. An exact chooser maximizing acceptance strictly prefers the first action under the same inequality. Turner and coauthors derive a tendency across reward functions from symmetries of an agent’s options. Here the common opportunity is survival under a fixed continuation, and the price test covers every end in its declared class. [Turner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01683), [R6.12](R6_unrepresented_values.md), [R6.13](R6_selection_learning.md).

**A choice even agents with opposite ends make.** In the W6 two-state world the actions choose A or B as next live state with the same current survival chance. The live-transition rows from A and B are $`(0.6,0.4)`$ and $`(0.1,0.6)`$. Their row sums are the chances of surviving each step. With two further steps the rows of $`K^2`$ are $`(0.40,0.48)`$ from A and $`(0.12,0.40)`$ from B. Even an agent that accepts **only B** at the end does better starting in A. Across all ends the advantage ranges from $`6/5`$ to $`10/3`$. An extra risk strictly below one in six leaves A better for everyone. With just one further step the worst-case ratio is $`2/3`$. As the assessment recedes the ratio tends to $`2`$ for every end. This gives the essay’s distinction a quantitative meaning. A can be pursued at an immediate cost for arbitrarily distant and very different purposes.

[R6.12’s action-copying experiment](R6_unrepresented_values.md#5-implemented-transfer-into-a-controller) gives the result an agent-level interpretation. External survival first changes the frequency of actions among retained trials. Copying those actions changes what a new controller does in fresh trials. In that example the limiting copy raises 32-step survival from A from 0.00119 to 0.01761. An informed planner free to choose A every time achieves 1. Copying transfers a disposition under the physical dynamics. Expressing it under a *new* toll requires selection or planning with that toll, or a new test of the deployed copy.

**A new deployment check.** A small controller learned two scores from survival flags alone. Starting in A, it trained with 4,096 attempts per first destination and ten subsequent baseline steps. It stored the success frequency for each destination with one success and one failure added for smoothing. At deployment it chose the larger stored score after dividing A’s by the newly offered price $`d`$. The price rule was supplied. We evaluated each deployed action against its exact chance of surviving and finishing in A or B.

| New price on A | Controllers choosing A, out of 16 seeds | Mean success probability forgone, end A | Same, end B |

| $`3/2`$ | 16 | 0 | 0 |
| $`2`$ | 9 | 0.000022938 | 0.000058982 |
| $`5/2`$ | 0 | 0 | 0 |

All 16 paid an extra one-third failure risk at $`d=3/2`$. For an end that rewards B alone, this raised the chance of surviving and finishing in B from 5.37% to 7.15%. Setting A’s stored score equal to B’s changed all 16 choices to B at that price. This shows where the acquired priority acts in this controller. The two ends enter only the evaluation. At $`d=2`$ their optimal choices differ. As a check on the small seed count, summing the two independent binomial training-count distributions gives probabilities of choosing A of 0.999998, 0.483176 and 0.000301 at the three respective prices. **What if an agent values some outcomes below death?** If exit pays a common amount $`D`$, write $`z_j=u_j-D`$ for the utility of living to state $`j`$ relative to exit. Then the expected payoff of the first action is $`D+(x/d)K^m z`$. Comparing with the second leaves $`(x/d-y)K^m z`$. If $`xh\ne d\,yh`$, the leading payoff difference has sign equal to the sign of $`(xh/d-yh)(v^\top z)`$. Thus an agent with $`v^\top z>0`$ eventually follows the strict long-run price comparison even when it regards some live outcomes as worse than exit. For $`v^\top z<0`$ its preference reverses, and at zero the smaller terms decide. A common horizon for a family of signed ends follows if their normalized margins $`v^\top z/\lVert z\rVert_\infty`$ stay above a positive constant. For the behavioral interpretation by retention, the acceptance weights must be nonnegative. For instance, take exit worth zero, A worth $`-1`$ and B worth $`+1`$. At no toll, diagonalizing the two-state matrix gives A’s advantage over B now as $`[(4/5)^m-9(2/5)^m]/4`$. This agent would reject A as its final destination and **prefers to enter A with four or more steps still to go**. Long-run success can align an early choice without giving the agents the same ultimate purpose.

**A continuing need and the price unit.** Multiplying every survival probability by a common $`c`$ with $`0<c<1`$ exposes **every state** to risk every round, even a state the agent can occupy forever. This multiplies $`K^m`$ by $`c^m`$ and leaves every relative price above unchanged. Yet when the leading eigenvalue is below one and $`0\le u_j\le1`$, both actions’ absolute endpoint payoffs tend to zero. A fixed additive fee for a single distant prize eventually exceeds the benefit. Conditioning on survival to increasingly late dates has a well-defined limit even when survival forever has probability zero.

An enduring need can also justify an **absolute** price. Suppose the agent can complete one unit of work it values on each future live transition under the W6 baseline. Its expected future work from each state is $`g=\sum_{n\ge1}K^n\mathbf1`$. The first-step equation $`g=K(\mathbf1+g)`$ gives $`g_A=17/3`$ and $`g_B=19/6`$. Entering A buys **2.5 additional expected units of work**. For any fixed fee below that difference, entering A pays once a uniformly bounded final assessment is sufficiently far away. If work also stops on the assessment date, its partial sum tends to the same $`g`$. Survival has this value through the work it makes possible. This comparison uses the original survival probabilities and baseline control after entry.

**Why a clamped end can still govern the choice.** Consider three states A, B, C with survival $`(1,1,3/5)`$. Baseline movements from A and B are $`(3,1,1)/5`$ and $`(1,3,1)/5`$. At C the baseline mixes two actions equally. Conditional on survival, left gives $`(3,1,4)/8`$, right gives $`(1,3,4)/8`$. The whole live chain mixes and $`h=(2,2,1)`$ has eigenvalue $`9/10`$. At C both actions have the same limiting value. Their live rows differ by $`(3/20)(1,-1,0)`$, a left eigenvector direction of $`K`$ with eigenvalue $`2/5`$. After $`m`$ more steps, left therefore beats right for the end “alive in A” by exactly $`(3/20)(2/5)^m`$. Right wins by the identical amount for “alive in B”. Survival alone is equal. The agents make opposite, strict choices at *every finite horizon* despite permanent pressure. The margin vanishes. Mixing washes out finite differences in retained odds, while exact maximization can still read the sign. A positive limiting action margin makes the common choice robust to finite precision.

If the world splits into isolated regions, an endpoint can select an entire region even far from its assessment. Communication restores the conclusion on a common primitive component once the relevant rows become positive. Slow mixing can delay agreement beyond any chosen finite test. The limit above concerns the interiors of longer finite trials. Actual assessments continue to distinguish ends at the boundary. A world with no assessment at all needs its own rule for evaluating infinite histories.

## 2. What the peaks of the value function measure

**2. A peak identifies the best long-run starting prospect under the declared baseline.** Write $`V_i=\log h_i`$. Its additive constant is arbitrary and all its pairwise differences are fixed. Indeed, $`(K^m\mathbf1)_i/(K^m\mathbf1)_j\to h_i/h_j`$. For the limiting retained movement $`Q`$, these same differences govern a choice between successors $`j,k`$ from the same state $`i`$:

```math
Q_{ij}=\frac{P_{ij}h_j}{(Ph)_i},\qquad
\log\frac{Q_{ij}/Q_{ik}}{P_{ij}/P_{ik}}=V_j-V_k .
```

The second equality follows by division. A higher neighbor in the essay’s landscape receives a larger increase in its odds relative to a lower neighbor. Absolute movement frequencies still depend on the opportunities in $`P`$. In W6’s two-state world $`h=(2,1)`$, but the selected process spends half its stationary time in each state.

*When does the peak agree with a survival planner’s destination?* Suppose a planner can choose any next live state at each step with free movement and survival paid in the current state. For two or more remaining steps, it can maximize survival by choosing a state of largest $`s`$ and choosing it again. If all baseline rows are equal, $`Kh=\lambda h`$ makes $`h`$ proportional to $`s`$, so the peaks agree. In fact, in any two-state positive baseline with unequal survival they agree. At a maximum of a nonconstant $`h`$, positivity gives $`(Ph)_i<h_i`$, hence $`s_i>\lambda`$. At a minimum it gives $`s_i<\lambda`$. In two states these inequalities decide the ordering. Equal survival everywhere gives a constant $`h`$ and reproduces the baseline behavior.

In three states the highest long-run prospect can be only moderately safe today. For example, take

```math
P=\frac1{10}\begin{pmatrix}8&1&1\\1&1&8\\1&1&8\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s=(5/9,1,1/3).
```

Multiplication gives $`Kh=h/2`$ for $`h=(4,3,1)`$. B is safe today, but baseline movement from B often leads to C. A is the best starting point if baseline behavior will resume, while repeated free choice lets the planner stay in B. The meaningful peak depends on who controls later moves.

The author’s other project makes the generality of this distinction clear. A score can encode any finite deterministic choice by putting the chosen action first. A sampler can reproduce positive action probabilities using their logarithms as scores. To predict choices among *new lotteries*, a utility over consequences must explain those choices through their expected consequences under a specified continuation. A high-level description such as “seeks safety” is well defined once its action summaries, menus and test distribution are declared. Behavior measured on those menus can leave preferences over other courses of events open. In discounted decision models another freedom is exact: adding $`\gamma\Phi(j)-\Phi(i)`$ to a transition reward shifts each policy’s value at $`i`$ by $`-\Phi(i)`$, by telescoping for bounded $`\Phi`$ and $`0<\gamma<1`$. This can move state maxima while preserving all policy rankings from a fixed start. The much tighter identification of $`V`$ above comes from the known baseline and the particular rule that turns it into $`Q`$. [Policy reconstruction and utility factorization](https://github.com/TristanMiano/policy_value_isomorph/blob/5173cb0433e10e36a2cc1138a82fade0df91a13f/docs/policy_reconstruction_vs_utility_factorization.md), [selection semantics](https://github.com/TristanMiano/policy_value_isomorph/blob/5173cb0433e10e36a2cc1138a82fade0df91a13f/docs/selection_semantics.md), [R6.15 on shaping](R6_value_use.md#2-a-value-representation-that-preserves-successful-action).

## 3. Which part came from the environment?

**3. A running end and survival have an identifiable combined effect.** Let $`r_i`$ be a fixed running incentive in log-weight units. Weight paths by the product of $`w_i=s_i e^{r_i}`$ along their successive departure states. The same endpoint, state sequence and baseline give identical path weights for every decomposition with that product. Weights above one are relative weights. A common rescaling at each step makes them acceptance probabilities at fixed horizon without changing retained choices.

In the control interpretation, actions choose a next-state distribution $`q`$ and pay its relative entropy $`D(q\Vert P_i)`$ with respect to the baseline. Substitution of $`w`$ in the positive-eigenvector equation gives

```math
\log\lambda+V_i=\log s_i+r_i+
\max_q\left\{\sum_j q_j V_j-D(q\Vert P_i)\right\}.
```

Subtracting the expression at $`q`$ from its maximum gives $`D(q\Vert Q_i)`$, so the same $`Q`$ uniquely maximizes each row. Stationary averaging cancels the two $`V`$ terms. The resulting objective is mean log survival plus mean running incentive less mean control cost. This establishes the fusion for this specific action space and cost. An agent could compute the rule from a stored $`r`$, or learn the identical policy by copying. A selective, nonconstant edit of the stored reward followed by exact replanning at fixed survival changes the optimal rule. Indeed, the inverse below would recover the same relative combined weights if the rule were unchanged. Such an edit tests the role of the stored reward. [R6.12’s control derivation](R6_unrepresented_values.md#3-a-value-representation-and-an-exact-control-comparison).

*What can be learned while the pressure stays on?* With known positive $`P`$ and a limiting row of $`Q`$ under this model, $`Q_{ij}/P_{ij}`$ recovers $`h_j`$ up to a common factor. Then $`h_j/(Ph)_j=w_j/\lambda`$ recovers all relative combined weights. At a single pressure level any change $`s'_j=s_j e^{b_j}`$ within $`(0,1]`$, paired with $`r'_j=r_j-b_j`$, preserves the entire combined model. Independent observations of survival before selection can supply the relative $`s_j`$ and thereby the differences in running incentives.

There is also a way to separate them using behavior while survival matters in **every** observation. Give the same live state one or two independent survival checks before moving, with the same $`r`$ paid once and the same baseline. For state $`j`$ and reference state $`k`$, let $`z_1,z_2`$ be the log ratios of inferred combined weights in the two conditions. Then

```math
z_b=b\log(s_j/s_k)+(r_j-r_k),\qquad
\log(s_j/s_k)=z_2-z_1,\qquad r_j-r_k=2z_1-z_2 .
```

The identification uses the known change in exposure and a controller or retained population at the corresponding limiting rule in each condition. A known multiplier on each state’s unknown survival, even a different multiplier per state, preserves the ambiguity between models with the same original product. The power intervention changes the coefficient of the unknown log survival. If each inferred log ratio has error at most $`e`$, the two linear combinations give errors at most $`2e`$ in the log survival ratio and $`3e`$ in the running-incentive difference. Allowing the running reward to change freely with exposure would destroy that identification. With one permanently fixed exposure and no measurement of its components, the combined effect is the identifiable answer. Even two exposures recover a running weight under this model. Evidence that a particular agent stores it requires a causal or internal observation.

The assumed decoder can itself be tested. When several rows are observed, a common field exists precisely if the rows of $`(Q_{ij}/P_{ij})`$ are proportional. Necessity follows from the row formula in statement 2. For sufficiency take a common positive row shape $`h`$; normalization of each row of $`Q`$ fixes its multiplier at $`1/(Ph)_i`$. With an unknown baseline this restriction can disappear. For any chosen positive field $`h`$, taking $`P_{ij}`$ proportional within each row to $`Q_{ij}/h_j`$ reproduces the same $`Q`$.

For example, in the two-state baseline the exact probability $`Q_{AA}`$ is $`3/4`$ under one exposure and $`9/11`$ under two in the [worked calibration](../notebook/W6_terminality_addendum/check_terminality.py). These observations and $`P`$ recover $`s_B/s_A=22/27`$ and $`e^{r_B-r_A}=189/220`$. The single-exposure inverse is the known passive-dynamics inverse-control problem. Dvijotham and Todorov also give an estimator from sampled transitions. The extra step here uses the two stipulated positive exposures to separate the factors. [Dvijotham and Todorov, §§2.1–2.2](https://icml.cc/Conferences/2010/papers/571.pdf).

An independent clamped end can survive the common pressure *intact*. Suppose the state has two coordinates with independent baselines $`P_X,P_Y`$, survival $`s(x)`$ and a running end $`r(y)`$. The weighted matrix is the product on pairs of $`\mathrm{diag}(s)P_X`$ and $`\mathrm{diag}(e^r)P_Y`$. Multiplying their positive eigenvectors pointwise on pairs gives the product eigenvector. Substitution in the row formula gives $`Q_X(x,x')Q_Y(y,y')`$. The first coordinate responds to the common pressure, and the entire choice law on the second is exactly what the end alone would produce. A controller with this control objective and these separable opportunities can keep expressing its distinct purpose indefinitely. Shared pressure can thus explain a shared part of what agents do while leaving an independent part to their ends.

## 4. How fixed does a useful priority become while support continues?

**4. Persistent support can stabilize both the priority and its expression across generations.** Take the two program types in R6.4. Type 1 acts on the priority, type 0 lacks it. Each generation type 1 leaves $`R>1`$ times as much descendant weight, and a transmitted program flips its type with probability $`0<\mu<1/2`$ in either direction. The rules stay fixed. The fraction holding the priority approaches $`q_*>1/2`$. Among the ancestors of distant survivors, how often does an already acquired priority get revised away while this pressure stays on?

Write the descendant matrix and its leading eigenpair as

```math
M=\begin{pmatrix}1-\mu&\mu\\R\mu&R(1-\mu)\end{pmatrix},
\qquad Mh=\Lambda h,\qquad H=h_1/h_0 .
```

Scaling $`M`$ by $`1/R`$ makes it exactly the live kernel of R6.12, with survival $`(1/R,1)`$ and symmetric baseline revision. This gives a direct mathematical connection between the two W6 constructions. Follow a history forward in time after sampling it by its mass among descendants at a much later date. This model propagates deterministic path weights. In the limiting retained history, the probability of a switch away from type 1 is

```math
b=\frac{\mu}{\mu+(1-\mu)H}.
```

For fixed $`\mu`$, increasing the ongoing advantage $`R`$ strictly increases $`H`$ and decreases $`b`$. Thus the *same physical revision rate* produces longer uninterrupted runs of a priority in successful histories when its usefulness grows. At any finite $`R`$, the typical retained lineage eventually switches. The unbroken-run probability for $`t`$ further transitions of the limiting chain is $`(1-b)^t`$.

*Proof.* The right-eigenvector equations give
$`\mu H^2-(R-1)(1-\mu)H-R\mu=0`$. The quadratic has one positive root, exceeds zero for large $`H`$ and is negative at $`H=1`$. Increasing $`R`$ lowers its value at every positive $`H`$, moving the positive root strictly upward. Dividing the two entries of row 1 after weighting successors by $`h`$ gives $`b`$. Successive steps of the limiting Markov chain then give the run probability. Forward descendant frequencies converge to the normalized **left** eigenvector. Their mean reproductive weight obeys $`\Lambda=1+(R-1)q_*`$. [R6.4, sections 3 and 5](R6_plastic_priorities.md).

For $`R=2`$, $`\mu=0.02`$, about **96.08%** of the current population holds the priority. Among newly transmitted programs from a type-1 parent, **2%** flip. Among type-1 ancestors sampled through distant surviving descendants, only **0.0416%** flip at the next step, giving a mean waiting time of about **2,404** transitions until the first switch from type 1. Here $`H\approx49.04`$ is also the limiting relative descendant weight of starting with the priority instead of without it. This long-run pattern exists with the pressure continually on. A model with a bank of competing programs inside one agent needs an actual update and choice rule to give their weights control over its actions.

The difference between forward transmission and the history of a successful lineage is established in work on ancestral types. Georgii and Baake identify a retrospective process in multitype branching. Baake, Di Gaspero and Cordero quantify the bias of mutations on ancestral lines in a finite Moran population. Here the smaller finite matrix lets us put a revision probability and a price of choosing the priority on the same scale $`H`$. [Georgii and Baake](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0302049), [Baake, Di Gaspero and Cordero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03512).

These observations have different identifying power. A stationary population fraction alone leaves $`R`$ and $`\mu`$ ambiguous. If both rows of the limiting ancestral transition matrix $`Q`$ are observed and revision is known to be symmetric, the diagonal entries give $`R=Q_{11}/Q_{00}`$. The two off-diagonal entries give $`\mu/(1-\mu)=\sqrt{Q_{01}Q_{10}/(Q_{00}Q_{11})}`$ by cancellation of the $`h`$ factors. Here longitudinal observations can identify revisability without removing the pressure. With asymmetric forward rates, even complete ancestral rows can agree between populations with $`R=10/7`$ and $`R=9/5`$. The checker constructs both. With only a short record of unchanged choices, an installed bias and a continually renewed disposition may look identical. Even if removal is possible, R6.4 gives $`q_t-1/2=(q_0-1/2)(1-2\mu)^t`$. For $`D=|q_0-1/2|>0`$, $`T\ge1`$ and measurement tolerance $`0<\delta<D`$, every positive rate at most $`[1-(1-\delta/D)^{1/T}]/2`$ keeps the entire removal trajectory within $`\delta`$ of an unchanged fraction through time $`T`$. This is a statement about a declared precision and window.

**How strong must a lasting pressure be?** With fixed $`0<\mu<1/2`$, a continuing lower bound $`R_t\ge R_*>1`$ keeps the long-run priority share at least $`q_*(R_*,\mu)>1/2`$, by comparison of the increasing one-step maps. If $`R_t=1+1/(t+1)`$, usefulness stays positive at every date, yet $`q_t\to1/2`$. Indeed selection adds at most $`(R_t-1)/4`$ to the current share since $`q(1-q)\le1/4`$. Symmetric revision then contracts the distance to $`1/2`$ by $`1-2\mu`$. Taking the upper limit yields a distance of zero. With zero revision and both types initially present, the exact same pressures multiply their odds by $`\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}R_t=m+1`$. The result depends on continuing strength relative to the rate of revision.

If revision rates themselves are inherited, R6.4 proves that $`\Lambda(\mu)`$ decreases strictly with $`\mu`$ under this constant advantage. This is a two-type instance of the established reduction principle for transmission. [Altenberg, Liberman and Feldman](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5373362/). In a finite repertoire of separately reproducing rates, the lowest available rate dominates asymptotically if the favored type is represented there. If that rate is zero, the copying channel blocks priority flips. A positive minimum leaves the channel open. A sequence of rates approaching zero can make transmission arbitrarily faithful. With ongoing irreducible mutation between rates, including some positive rates, descendants can leave zero. In Fable’s finite example all rates communicate and the priority can flip at positive rates. The joint matrix is irreducible with self-loops, so its normalized composition converges to a positive Perron vector. At update 20,000 its rate-zero share is **0.6811** and its priority-holding share **0.9989**. In R6.4’s reversal example, alternating which priority is useful every five updates gives growth **2.713459** at revision rate 0.2 versus **2** at zero. Faithful inheritance of a constantly useful priority and flexible response to changing usefulness thus have different supporting conditions. [Fable’s probe](../notebook/W6_terminality_addendum/probe.py), [R6.4’s comparison](R6_plastic_priorities.md#5-persistent-usefulness-can-favor-making-the-priority-harder-to-revise).


**An effective clamp can emerge from selection alone, even starting with nobody expressing the priority.** Give each program a faithfully inherited, strictly positive revision rate. In the deterministic descendant-mass model, seed countably many such rates approaching zero, each with positive mass and finite total mass. Every program may start as type 0. Keep the same $`R>1`$ and symmetric flip law. Then the fraction expressing the priority tends to one and the physical probability of revision in the next transmission tends to zero. Every actual program retains a positive revision rate.

The fixed ingredients here are the environment, inheritance of the revision rate and an update channel that can produce the priority. Selection changes the mix of available programs. The origin of those update rules is a separate question. In an exact check with $`R=2`$, rates $`2^{-k}`$ and initial masses $`2^{1-k}`$ for $`k\ge2`$, adoption reaches **70.08% after 8 generations and 99.21% after 512**, starting with no adoption. Among the next generation’s transmissions the probability of a physical flip is **0.3945%**. The computation bounds the omitted infinite tail. A finite repertoire also achieves any specified approximation if its positive minimum is sufficiently small. For instance, at $`R=2`$ the stationary share $`q_*=0.99`$ requires only $`\mu=99/19700`$, obtained by substituting $`q_*`$ in the one-step update.

A seeded rate $`\delta<\epsilon`$ contributes at least a positive constant times $`\Lambda(\delta)^t`$, using the reverse eigenvector bound at that one rate. Consequently the mean physical revision rate tends to zero, including after reproduction reweights parents by factors between 1 and $`R`$.

Write $`Z_t`$ for total descendant mass and $`q_t`$ for its priority share. The same lower bound with $`\delta`$ arbitrarily small, together with $`Z_t\le R^t Z_0`$, gives $`Z_t^{1/t}\to R`$. **Can a priority actually stop changing if no switch is ever forbidden?** Yes, along a realized history. Suppose the same advantage $`R>1`$ continues and the physical revision probability $`0<\mu_t<1/2`$ tends to zero. Write $`F(q)=Rq/[1+(R-1)q]`$ for the fraction expressing the priority just after selection. The next fraction is a mixture of $`F(q_t)`$ and $`1/2`$, with weight $`2\mu_t`$ on $`1/2`$. From any positive starting share, $`q_t\ge\min(q_0,1/2)>0`$ since $`F(q)\ge q`$. As $`\mu_t\to0`$, the difference $`q_{t+1}-F(q_t)`$ vanishes. Continuity and monotonicity give $`\liminf q_t=F(\liminf q_t)`$, whose only positive solution is 1. Almost the entire population eventually holds the priority.

Now follow one forward transmission per generation, choosing its successor before weighting by later descendants. Its probability of no more switches from time $`T`$ is $`\prod_{t\ge T}(1-\mu_t)`$. If $`\mu_t=1/(t+2)^2`$, this product telescopes to $`(T+1)/(T+2)`$. With probability one there is a last switch or none at all, even though every future step still permits revision. If $`\mu_t=1/(t+3)`$, the product is zero for every $`T`$ and the line switches infinitely often with probability one. **Both schedules approach 100% population prevalence under the same permanent advantage.** Their implications for whether a particular priority ever settles are opposite. These schedules are prescribed. In the preceding selection construction, each individual line instead keeps its own positive physical rate and remains revisable forever. The rate distribution among its contemporaries is what changes.

## A test W7 can carry forward

How could we tell that acquiring a priority changed what an agent values *in itself*? Keep the environmental demand active. Offer two routes to the same future lottery, one of which expresses the priority and carries a small fee payable for sure. For example, both routes can have the same effective protection because a backup is already present. Observe whether the agent buys the extra protection. Check what the agent *expects* from each route. A controller trained on the old correlation may still pay because it expects the old benefit.

There is a quantitative interpretation. Suppose the controller values route $`i`$ by a direct premium $`v_i`$ plus its expected utility of the declared downstream consequences, less its fee. Its downstream utility has range at most $`B`$. Its two predictive lotteries have total variation distance at most $`\eta`$, and its choice is within $`\epsilon`$ utility units of optimal. If it buys route A at fee $`f`$ when B is free, then

```math
v_A-v_B \ \ge\ f-\epsilon-B\eta .
```

Indeed the largest expected downstream advantage allowed by that distance is $`B\eta`$. Choosing A spends at most another $`\epsilon`$ in decision error. For example, paying $`0.10`$ with $`B=1`$, $`\eta=0.02`$ and $`\epsilon=0.01`$ requires a direct premium of at least $`0.07`$ in that model. If it declines at a higher fee $`g`$ with the same beliefs, the corresponding upper bound is $`g+\epsilon+B\eta`$. Together these bounds put the premium on a declared scale. Changing which consequences are counted downstream can change the attributed premium. When every feasible action couples survival to another valued outcome, choices can leave their attribution unresolved. Independent access to the agent’s forecasts, or reliable elicitation of them, matters as much as matching the physical lotteries.

This is a proposed test, additional to the implemented training and score intervention in §1. It distinguishes *acting on a learned priority*, which that controller demonstrates, from *still paying when the agent knows the other benefits are matched*. For W7, track the deployed controller’s choices and forecasts as well as the rates in **all** transmissions and in the ancestors of later survivors. In the stated models those observations test different mechanisms contributing to an acquired preference, faithful copying and amplification by the environment. Which encoded ends spread when they conflict, and what makes an agent revise them, remain W7.6 questions.
