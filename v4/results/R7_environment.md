# R7.1 · When a community changes the environment that selects its priorities

A community can preserve two useful priorities and still fail to staff the two services people need. In the example below, increasing the number of independent recommendations required to train an apprentice changes stable provision into alternating shortages. Both regimes have exactly the right *average* proportions. Which tasks actually get done distinguishes them.

Codex (GPT-6), P4-R7-C001. The mathematical results give a mechanism that can produce different outcomes under the same external demand. Two bird archives let us follow actual performers across changes of partner or roster. In eight complete turnover aviaries an 87.48-point increase in use of an efficient technique includes 4.41 points from changing choices by continuing demonstrators. Together the comparisons address which mechanisms can select a practice and where change was actually observed.

## From performance to transmission

There are two jobs with equal demand. Each mentor acts on an installed preference for one of them. With share $x$ in the first job, a client needing that job chooses uniformly among its providers. Each apprentice independently collects $m$ referrals and accepts a mentor if they all nominate the same person. Initially suppose enough referrals are available to repeat until a place is filled. Copying the mentor's priority flips it with probability $0<\mu<1/2$. The client's choice, the criterion for accepting a mentor, and the new worker's internal ranking are three different operations.

In a classroom of size $N$ with both jobs represented, an individual provider receives a referral with probability $1/(2Nx)$ or $1/[2N(1-x)]$. Raising this to the power $m$ and summing over providers gives the two supplies of qualifying mentors. Their ratio is $[x/(1-x)]^{1-m}$. Write $y$ for the first job's share among qualifying mentors. The new share in the deterministic mass model is $\mu+(1-2\mu)y$. With $N$ independent apprentices this is instead their common Bernoulli probability. Once a job has no mentor a referral is reassigned to the service available; a copying error can reintroduce the missing priority.

**Stable diversity and endogenous alternation.** For $m\ge1$, every interior deterministic trajectory approaches balanced staffing if $(m-1)(1-2\mu)\le1$. Above that threshold, every initially unbalanced trajectory approaches the same pair of alternating shares, up to phase. The outside demand and copying error stay constant.

*Proof.* In log odds $l$ the map is $-h((m-1)l)$, where $h(z)=2\,\mathrm{atanh}((1-2\mu)\tanh(z/2))$. With $a=1-2\mu$ and $t=\tanh(z/2)$ its derivative is $a(1-t^2)/(1-a^2t^2)$. Thus $h$ is odd, bounded, increasing and strictly concave on $z>0$. If the slope at zero is at most one, iteration of the absolute log odds decreases to zero. If it exceeds one, strict concavity and boundedness give exactly one positive fixed magnitude. The increasing magnitude map takes every positive starting point monotonically toward it, while the sign changes each generation. At $m=1$ all starting points reach balance in one step.

With three independent referrals, $z=2x-1$ obeys $z'=-2(1-2\mu)z/(1+z^2)$. At $\mu=3/16$ the attracting shares are $1/4$ and $3/4$. If there is one unit of labor per unit of total demand and each worker can serve only their own job, the fraction of demand served is $1-|x-1/2|$. It averages **75% on the cycle** and **100% at balance**, although the long-run mean share in the first job is $1/2$ in both. By contrast, at $N=8$ a triple of referrals agrees on one mentor with probability $5/144$ at either cycle share, above $1/64$ at balance. An organization rewarded solely for agreements can register an improvement while leaving more requests unanswered.

## When the environment follows the population

Requests can respond to yesterday’s workers. Suppose the odds of requesting the first service equal its provider odds raised to $\beta\ge0$. This changes the demand in the referral experiment, leaving the outside evaluator free to measure service to fixed original needs. Pre-error mentor odds are supply odds to the power $k=1-m+m\beta$. The same proof, now with signed update $h(kl)$, gives three regimes. If $a|k|\le1$ both starting majorities return to balance. For $ak>1$ there are two attracting stationary majorities, with the initial majority determining which priority persists. For $ak<-1$ both priorities persist in an alternating shortage. With $m=3,\mu=3/16$, keeping the requests fixed gives the $1/4,3/4$ cycle. Letting request odds respond with $\beta=4/3$ instead makes the very same two proportions *stable conventions in different communities*. This is selection on what people come to request, which can be measured separately from the capacity to supply both original needs.

Endogenous demand and inheritance have distinct effects. As a perturbation, suppose a mentor acts on its encoded ranking with probability $(1+\kappa)/2$ and does the other job otherwise. An apprentice can copy either the actual ranking or only the observed job. At balanced fixed demand, the derivative of the next ranking share with respect to the current ranking share at $1/2$ is $a(1-m\kappa^2)$ for transmitted rankings. To see this, a shift in the type share changes job supply by $\kappa$ times as much. The log ratio of per-provider nomination weights changes by $-4m\kappa$ times the original shift. The two types have overlapping job choices, giving a second factor of $\kappa$ in their relative return. The prior log odds contribute $4$; conversion back to a share contributes $1/4$, and mutation contributes $a$. Copying the observed job gives slope $a(1-m)\kappa$ instead by substitution in the service-share map. For $m=3,\mu=3/16,\kappa=0.85$, direct transmission returns locally to balance while action copying locally amplifies alternation. An observation of what mentors *do* needs a transmission experiment to establish what their apprentices have inherited.

**The price of acquiring agreement matters.** Let the apprentice try at most $B$ independent referral groups and copy a uniformly drawn existing mentor if all fail. At balance the agreement probability per attempt is $Z=N^{1-m}$, so $A=1-(1-Z)^B$ is the success probability. With fixed demand the derivative of the next share at balance becomes $a(1-mA)$. The derivative of $A$ drops out there because both success and fallback yield the same share. With $N=8,m=3,\mu=3/16$, at least **128** attempts per place are needed for this local instability. At $N=64$ it takes **8,253**. Repeated referrals can produce the strong feedback in the first result, but its physical cost depends sharply on classroom size and the fallback. These thresholds concern local response; they make no assertion about all large perturbations.

## Which process is doing the learning?

The same relative success $r$ can update a transmitted population and the competing dispositions *within* a person. Let learner $i$ begin with propensity $p_i$ for the first action and let weights $w_i$ sum to one. Given full feedback proportional to $(r,1)$, a normalized multiplicative update sends $p$ to $g(p)=rp/[1+(r-1)p]$. Pooling dispositions before selection gives $g(\bar p)$ with $\bar p=\sum_iw_i p_i$. Updating each person and averaging gives a different answer measured by

$$
g(\bar p)-\sum_i w_i g(p_i)
=\frac{r(r-1)}{\bar d^2}\sum_i\frac{w_i(p_i-\bar p)^2}{d_i},
\qquad d_i=1+(r-1)p_i,\quad \bar d=\sum_i w_i d_i.
$$

For $r\ne1$ substitute $g(p)=r(1-1/d)/(r-1)$ and expand $1/d_i$ about $1/\bar d$ with exact quadratic remainder. At $r=1$ both sides vanish. Equal weights on $.1$ and $.9$, with $r=3$, give $17/28$ under local updating and $3/4$ under pooled copying. If learners themselves acquire carrier weights $w_i d_i/\bar d$, their updated average equals $g(\bar p)$ exactly. A common symmetric probability of revision after either update scales the discrepancy by $1-2\mu$.

This specifies a bridge to inheritance by differential reproduction, or to cultural transmission through successful teachers. The material that must be transmitted is the ranking or a procedure that reconstructs it. Within-person learning also requires an action decoder and the assumed feedback. A common average trajectory alone cannot identify those mechanisms. Tracking the same workers before and after feedback, randomizing teachers independently of parentage, and counting transmission weights distinguish causal predictions under these models.

**When can survivor selection alone explain an apparent rearing effect?** Within one population of origin, randomly assign rearing condition 0 or 1 and offer the same binary choice at a later assessment. Under condition $j$, let $r_j$ be the fraction seen and $p_j$ the fraction of those seen choosing A. Leaving, dying and giving no response may depend on the assignment. An unseen individual's choice need never be defined. Among *all originally assigned*, the fraction that would be seen in both conditions and change from B to A is at least
$$
\max\{0,\ r_1p_1+r_0(1-p_0)-1\}.
$$
Indeed the B-seen-under-0 and A-seen-under-1 groups must overlap by this much. The bound is sharp since the two event sets can be placed as far apart as their sizes permit, then each complement split to match its two residual categories. If independent tracking bounds the fraction seen only under 1 by $e$, the stronger lower bound $\max\{0,r_1p_1-r_0p_0-e\}$ follows by accounting for all the A observations under 1. This is a principal-stratification argument. [Frangakis and Rubin (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0006-341x.2002.00021.x), [parallel purchase bound and numerical check](R7_reasons.md).

For example, a shift from 30% to 70% A at 80% observation in each arm forces at least **12% of the original cohort** to change its standardized choice. A population attaining the bound consists of 20% seen only under 1 choosing A, 20% seen only under 0 choosing B, 24% always choosing each of A and B, and 12% making the switch. If at most 2% are seen only under 1, the guaranteed fraction rises to 30%. At 65% observation in both arms the same 30-to-70 contrast is possible with zero switchers. One witness has 26% seen only under each condition, 19.5% always choosing each option and 9% never seen. These are design calculations on exact population frequencies. Finite data need uncertainty bands, and a response to the rearing bundle can involve physical skill, social exposure or changed evaluation. The assessment asks which part of the apparent effect can survive the strongest demographic explanation.

## A built environment can preserve two conventions and an alternation at once

Material infrastructure, client habits or past training can outlive one cohort. Let $\ell_t$ be log provider odds as above and $z_t$ log demand odds sustained by the installed environment. The next cohort follows the same $m$-referral mechanism. The environment geometrically combines the previous installation's odds with a new signal supplied by the current workers:
$$
\ell_{t+1}=h((1-m)\ell_t+m z_t),\qquad
z_{t+1}=\rho z_t+(1-\rho)\beta\ell_t .
$$
Here $0\le\rho<1$ is persistence and $\beta$ specifies how strongly current service affects subsequent clients' relative demand. These are two different inherited states. Holding the new cohort fixed while changing the installation is an intervention on the second.

**Coexistence under a single ecology.** Take $m=2$, $\mu=3/16$, $\rho=1/2$, $\beta=3/2$. Write $g=\log 3$. The two constant states are $(g,3g/2)$ and its negative; another regime alternates between $(g,-g/2)$ and its negative. **All three regimes locally attract.** At a constant state the input to $h$ is twice the first coordinate; on the alternation it is minus twice that coordinate. Since $h(2g)=g$ and $h$ is odd, substitution proves the claims about the states. At any of them $h'=3/10$: the pre-error share is $.9$ or $.1$, the post-error share $.75$ or $.25$. The Jacobian has rows $(-.3,.6)$ and $(.75,.5)$. Its trace is $.2$, determinant $-.6$ and both eigenvalues have magnitude below one. The two-step derivative on the alternating path is its square. Strict attraction also persists under sufficiently small perturbations of the parameters, by local continuation of these hyperbolic points of the one- or two-step map. Instantaneous demand $z_t=\beta\ell_t$ with the same parameters has an increasing one-dimensional update; it cannot have this alternating cycle.

Start both communities at exactly three quarters in service one. With installation $3g/2$, three quarters work there again next period and about **91.14%** of client jobs can be filled. Transplant just the installation to $-g/2$. Now one quarter work there next period and **61.60%** of jobs can be filled on the alternating path. The coverage calculation uses the actual local demand $\sigma(z)$, giving $1-|x-\sigma(z)|$. This is a physical discriminator between transmission through an agent and transmission through conditions its predecessors left behind. Laland, Odling-Smee and Feldman develop the evolutionary consequences of organisms changing the resource conditions their descendants inherit. [Niche construction and resource renewal (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.96.18.10242).

In finite populations the persistence depends on the number of training places. A seeded transplant check changes only the initial installation and samples every apprentice. Use a public half-count per service to compute log odds for the next installation when no current worker represents a service. After 128 generations with 64 places, **424/1,000** runs started in the positive steady environment still had a positive majority in each of the last eight generations; **415/1,000** had a strictly alternating majority over those generations. Starting instead in the alternating environment, **776/1,000** strictly alternated. With 256 places, all 1,000 runs from each start displayed its own original pattern in that final window (Python 3.13.14, seeded binomial draws). These are finite-horizon classifications; the deterministic proof establishes local attraction. [Simulation and stability checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/infrastructure.py).

## When can the expressed priority stand in for its hidden carriers?

Consider one known environment. A latent type $u$ might include family endowment, learned teacher, or a rule for revising after experience. Public choices group latent types into visible classes $a$. Write $s_u>0$ for the expected number of surviving copies and $r_u$ for their probability distribution over next visible classes. For population $p$ the next visible distribution is $\sum_u p_u s_u r_u/\sum_u p_u s_u$. This counts descendants or teaching places; for a single person's learning one would instead specify transitions of that same person.

**Exact aggregation criterion.** All populations with the same visible shares give identical next visible shares if and only if either every $r_u$ is the same everywhere, or **both $s_u$ and $r_u$ are constant within each visible class**. For necessity, two pure types from one class must give the same $r_u$. When another class has a different offspring distribution, mix either of the first two types half-and-half with this same second-class type. The mixing weight on the first distribution in the next generation changes strictly with its $s_u$, forcing equal outputs within its class. Sufficiency follows by grouping the sum. When $s_u$ are all equal this is ordinary Markov-chain lumpability. If hidden types also build different future environments, the environmental transition must descend to the visible classes as well. [Kemeny and Snell, Theorem 6.3.2](https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/yaoy/Fall2011/Kemeny-Snell_Chapter6.3-4.pdf).

For example, two kinds of publicly A-preferring workers each pass A to 80% of their descendants but leave four versus one teaching places. A B worker leaves two places and passes A with probability .2. A population with half A and half B becomes **60% A or 40% A**, depending solely on which latent kind of A worker was present. Measuring transmission fidelity while ignoring the carriers' output would predict the same answer twice.

There is also an approximate criterion. Suppose the total-variation diameter of $r_u$ within each class is at most $\epsilon$, its global diameter is $D$, and the ratios of outputs within any class are at most $k\ge1$. Two populations with the same visible shares then differ after one step by at most $\epsilon+D(k-1)/(k+1)$ in total variation. Conditional offspring mixtures in a class differ by at most $\epsilon$. Normalized class weights change by multiplicative factors before normalization between $1/k$ and $k$. For factors in $[a,b]$, total variation from reweighting is at most $(\sqrt b-\sqrt a)/(\sqrt b+\sqrt a)$: at fixed mean factor, the mean absolute deviation is maximized at the two endpoints by convexity; maximize that two-point expression over its mean. Mixing distributions of diameter $D$ multiplies this weight error by at most $D$. This bound specifies what needs measuring to use public behavior as an approximate evolutionary state.

## A measured transmission chain

Sasaki and Biro compared homing routes of birds flying alone, in fixed pairs, or in pairs with one experienced member and a succession of newcomers. Later transmission-chain pairs flew more efficient routes than the controls. Valentini and coauthors used the same experiment to measure in each direction how much the partner’s past turns predicted a bird’s next turn beyond its own history. This comparison lets us ask whether the role “experienced demonstrator” alone predicts the direction of informational influence. [Original experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms15049), [turn-by-turn analysis](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.68653).

I reanalyzed the authors’ archived transfer-entropy estimates at their setting chosen for maximum total information transfer. Novice-to-experienced transfer exceeded the reverse on **185 of 343 flights**. Giving equal weight to each of the ten original chains, five had a larger novice-to-experienced mean. The mean across chains was 0.11950 bits for experienced-to-novice and 0.11973 in the reverse direction. At the authors’ other reported setting, 184 flights and six chain means favored the reverse direction. The 38 pairs change membership over generations; only eight original chains supplied fifth-generation observations. Counting each flight as an independent lineage would give a misleading precision.

The authors report that the direction changes with experience along the chain. Our mean novice-minus-experienced differences by generation (2 through 5, averaging flights within each pair first) were +.00227, +.00570, −.00306 and −.00644 bits. Among the eight chains observed at both endpoints the mean change was −.01034, with decreases in four of eight; at the second parameter setting the change was −.01903, with decreases in five. Following 28 actual birds from novice to experienced in the next pair, their outgoing predictive information increased in 13 cases at either setting. Role, experience, partner and generation change together. Aggregate reversal need not describe the history of every individual.

**A correction to the comparison data.** The archived surrogate table averages .11036 and .11111 bits in the two directions. Its generating function in `figshare/scripts/information.R` stores the focal pair's position *within generation* but compares that position with population-wide pair IDs to exclude the focal pair. The numbering coincides only in generation 2. Reconciling the archived bird identities shows that 2,439 of 29,035 rows compare the same pair; 249 even use the same release. All four information measures in these 249 rows exactly equal their real-flight values, covering every pair in generations 3–5. We excluded all 2,439 same-pair rows, averaged the remaining controls within each of the 343 focal flights, and weighted the flights equally. This gives **.11089 and .11152** versus real-flight means **.11929 and .11992**. Actual minus matched control is +.00840 bits in *each* direction, positive in 274/343 and 261/343 flights respectively. All ten original chains had positive average excess in both directions (ranges .00155–.01467 and .00301–.01178 bits). Their surrogate comparisons reuse flights across chains, so this count is descriptive. Matching further on training release number in different pairs leaves comparators for 342 flights. The two excesses are then +.00842 and +.00860 bits, positive on 273 and 258 flights. Equal training position does not assure identical weather or route history. The adjusted novice-minus-experienced contrasts still move from positive in generations 2–3 to negative in 4–5. This correction concerns these archive comparisons; the route-efficiency experiment measures a different outcome. Both series carry predictive information that is stronger in true pairs. The figures describe movement coordination in these sampled flights. Distinguishing a change of preference from response to a shared cue or a partner requires an additional behavioral intervention. No biological inheritance parameter is inferred. The small reanalysis reads the archived statistics and reconstructs chain membership. It does not refit the GPS models. [Data and code, Valentini et al.](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.14043362), [our script](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/transmission_data.py).

## Did individuals change their technique, or did different individuals demonstrate it?

Chimento, Alarcón-Nieto and Aplin studied 18 captive great-tit populations. During an initial diffusion period only the slower solution to a food puzzle was available. Both then became available. Nine populations kept a fixed roster; in nine, two of six birds were replaced at each of four weekly turnovers. The authors attribute the spread of the faster technique particularly to immigrants' adoption. In their publication archive we can observe *which bird performed which technique, how often it worked and when it entered or left*. Their solver criterion requires at least twenty recorded solutions over the bird's participating history. The following quantities describe demonstrated actions by these solvers. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.03.057), [publication data and code](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4644347).

For adjacent stages let $w_i$ be bird $i$'s fraction of solves and $p_i$ its efficient fraction when active. The group fraction is $\sum_i w_i p_i$. For every bird active twice, assign $\bar w_i\Delta p_i$ to changed choices and $\Delta w_i(\bar p_i-c)$ to changed activity, using arithmetic midpoints and the midpoint $c$ of the two group fractions. A bird active in just one stage contributes its signed $w_i(p_i-c)$. Split these appearances and disappearances according to documented dates of roster entry or exit at the boundary. The four terms sum exactly to the change because total activity weights sum to one at both dates. This centered decomposition is invariant to adding a constant to the scoring scale. It accounts for *where* a change appears; a newcomer's behavior may itself have been learned from residents.

| Group, weighted by actual demonstrations | Total change | Same birds changed technique | Continuing birds changed activity weight | Roster entry or exit | Started or stopped demonstrating with membership overlapping the boundary |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Nine fixed populations | +11.18 | +9.01 | +1.92 | 0.00 | +0.25 |
| Eight turnover populations solving in all five stages | +87.48 | +4.41 | +11.12 | +48.52 | +23.42 |

Entries are percentage points, summing the four transitions within each population before averaging populations equally. In seven of eight complete turnover populations, roster changes made a positive contribution, in the eighth it was zero. Weighting every active bird equally instead gives +85.50 total, including +17.61 from changed choices and +50.17 from roster change. This sensitivity matters for deciding whether to predict the next *worker* or the next *public demonstration*. In the ninth turnover population the technique of opening the box died out and the last two stages have no recorded solutions. Assigning its undefined final efficient fraction any value from zero to one places the nine-population mean change in **[77.76, 88.87]** percentage points. The absence of a solver is itself a material outcome. Two tutors were reused across experimental conditions; these counts support no independent-bird standard error.

The midpoint convention affects how much of the gain is assigned specifically to the roster. Allow a different reference fraction anywhere between the two observed group means at each transition. Each component is affine in that reference, so evaluating both ends at every transition gives sharp componentwise ranges. For turnover, the roster contribution can be **13.33–83.71 points**, with the continuing birds' change of technique still exactly **4.41 points**. Documented roster changes plus previously or subsequently silent demonstrators account for **about 59 points even at their joint minimum** over these conventions. This is stronger evidence about the composition of public demonstrations than about the exact division between replacement and changes of activity.

Do the same birds also experience the advantage *before* expressing a later preference? Among nontutor solvers, we matched the first ten uses of each technique by use number, retained measured times of 0–60 seconds and required at least three paired observations and a subsequent, nonoverlapping final tenth of choices. Only **eight birds** qualified. All three for whom the efficient technique was initially faster on average in log-one-plus seconds chose it on every recorded final-window trial. Two of the other five eventually chose it on a majority. Requiring five pairs leaves six birds and the corresponding counts are two of two and two of four. These few selected observations cannot establish what any individual valued internally. They show how thin an otherwise large event archive becomes for a question about an early experienced payoff followed by a later choice. [Exact decomposition and paired-use check](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/turnover_data.py).

Across the entire nontutor roster we also paired each bird's *first* 15 solves with its *last* 15, requiring seven days between the two windows. This asks about changes in an established bird, without weighting a prolific individual more highly. Of 118 turnover entries, 36 qualified. Sixteen favored the inefficient door in both windows, 19 favored the efficient door in both and **one** changed its majority toward efficiency. In static aviaries, none of 18 eligible birds changed majority (45 roster entries). Using five solves per window admits more birds and finds five changes toward efficiency among 43 eligible turnover birds and one among 23 static birds. No majority went in the reverse direction. None passed the 15-solve, two-sided conditional test for a personal change after charging .05 across all 163 roster entries. That test assumes exchangeability of the order of the two windows' observed choices given their total. An animal can learn a technique before its first success and still appear perfectly stable here.

Indeed the local demonstration history predicts *first* solves. To avoid counting acts before an entrant arrived, use only other birds' recorded solves on dates strictly after its entry day and earlier than its first solve. In turnover aviaries the majority of the last five predicted **56 of 67** first choices with sufficient history. It correctly predicted 30 of 31 where it favored the efficient door. Always predicting the seeded inefficient door gave 27/67. Using older demonstrations after entry disagreed with the recent majority nine times and was right once. A stronger forecast, the majority of first choices in other turnover aviaries in the same experiment week, already got **53/67**; recent local choices won only 11 of the 19 disagreements. Including the entry day gives 63/75 versus 60/75. Thus much of the predictability was shared across the experimental schedule. These records establish an opportunity for social learning before a bird had solved the puzzle itself. They neither locate the bird's attention at a particular demonstration nor establish its internal objective. [Marked-bird and entrance checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/turnover_data.py).

A separate field intervention changed the rearing environment independently of a bird's birth species. Slagsvold and Wiebe observed foraging by cross-fostered blue and great tits. Foraging height and use of twigs shifted toward those of the foster species. The height analysis found a species-by-treatment interaction in both autumn (180 observations in the analysis) and spring (101). This carries the comparison beyond behavioral similarity among biological relatives. It changes a bundle of early ecological and social experience; measuring direct copying of a particular adult would require a different intervention. [Slagsvold and Wiebe, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1679873/).

### When turnover helps an improved technique without losing its last carrier

In a separate six-worker model, one worker can demonstrate a technique that finishes a job by its deadline. Choose $b$ roster places for replacement. If no demonstrated source survives the exchange, the group cannot reconstruct the technique unaided. Otherwise each surviving user retains it, a remaining nonuser learns with probability $s$ and a receptive newcomer with probability $t$, independently. From one source, random removal gives expected trained workers $(1-b/6)[1+(5-b)s+bt]$. At $s=.05,t=.9$, the best number of random replacements is two for next-round use, one for use in round seven, and zero for use in round 28. With two replacements, 32.78% of workers use the technique in the first round but only 65.31% of groups still have a source by round 28. This separates rapid adoption from continuity of the ability to learn.

An institution can instead reserve a randomly chosen demonstrator until the new roster is in place, filling the other places as before. Assume it can check the deadline on an actual common job. The new roster then always has a source. With two replacements, 49.17% use the technique next round and 92.25% do by round 28. Continuity requires **no permanent demonstrator**. For $1\le b<6$ a current sole source has a positive chance of training a second source this round. The next round can reserve that source and replace the first one. The probabilities have a uniform positive lower bound over the finite states, so every named incumbent eventually leaves almost surely. Under random removal with $b>0$ and $s,t<1$, there is a positive-probability path from every roster to zero sources by repeatedly removing trained workers while no one learns. Finiteness gives eventual loss almost surely. Voluntary handover, a durable worked example, or fresh discovery would change this conclusion; only the first is modeled here. The published aviaries illustrate what is at stake; their data were not used to fit these transition probabilities. [Finite transition calculation](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/handover.py).

## Evidence and comparisons

In the fixed-demand referral model with finitely many workers and positive copying error, all population counts communicate, so their stationary distribution is unique. Exchange of job names makes its mean share exactly one half. For 64 training places, one, two and three referrals give stationary demand coverage of **95.03%, 93.64% and 77.91%**. The respective lag-one correlations of the share are approximately **0, −0.625 and −0.967**. These are numerical stationary calculations from the finite transition matrix. At eight places the checker also enumerates actual referral tuples independently. [Executable](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/service.py).

Environmental feedback, coexisting types and cycling have established accounts in evolutionary games. Chotibut and coauthors already show how equilibrium time-average loads can conceal poor service under multiplicative learning in a routing game. Here repeated client referrals implement the feedback strength, and the carrier-weight calculation tells when interpreting it as individual learning succeeds. [Tilman, Plotkin and Akçay](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14531-6), [Chotibut et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02486), [Harper on replicator weighting and inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1763).

Chimento and colleagues also studied optimal turnover in populations of reinforcement learners. Their model explains how repertoire composition and valuation by experienced individuals affect the output of better techniques. Our six-worker chain isolates a different design choice a manager can actually make: retaining one verified demonstrator until replacements can learn. Its contribution is the tradeoff between immediate service and losing the last carrier, alongside the event-level accounting above. [Chimento et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1086/730110).

**Aim-balance self-review.** Starting with an aggregate selection equation risked giving individual learning credit for a change of personnel. The identified individuals, explicit training weights and independent intervention on inherited infrastructure give that reading tests it can pass or fail. Keeping the lost technique in the denominator and comparing recent demonstrations with a calendar-only prediction reduced the apparent empirical support for local copying. Checking first choices also revealed where useful transmission could have occurred *before* an individual's own trial history began. A next test would vary access to a demonstrator while preserving the roster, task and recorded outcomes; it could distinguish individual acquisition from differential retention under the same cultural pressure.
