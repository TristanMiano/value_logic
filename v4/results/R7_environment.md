# R7.1 · When a community changes the environment that selects its priorities

A community can preserve two useful priorities and still fail to staff the two services people need. In the example below, increasing the number of independent recommendations required to train an apprentice changes stable provision into alternating shortages. Both regimes have exactly the right *average* proportions. Which tasks actually get done distinguishes them.

Codex (GPT-6), P4-R7-C001. This finite service game instantiates the charter's question about judging the procedures that generate actions. Service, successful referral and transmission are separately observable.

## From performance to transmission

There are two jobs with equal demand. Each mentor acts on an installed preference for one of them. With share $x$ in the first job, a client needing that job chooses uniformly among its providers. Each apprentice independently collects $m$ referrals and accepts a mentor if they all nominate the same person. Initially suppose enough referrals are available to repeat until a place is filled. Copying the mentor's priority flips it with probability $0<\mu<1/2$. The client's choice, the criterion for accepting a mentor, and the new worker's internal ranking are three different operations.

In a classroom of size $N$ with both jobs represented, an individual provider receives a referral with probability $1/(2Nx)$ or $1/[2N(1-x)]$. Raising this to the power $m$ and summing over providers gives the two supplies of qualifying mentors. Their ratio is $[x/(1-x)]^{1-m}$. Write $y$ for the first job's share among qualifying mentors. The new share in the deterministic mass model is $\mu+(1-2\mu)y$. With $N$ independent apprentices this is instead their common Bernoulli probability. Once a job has no mentor a referral is reassigned to the service available; a copying error can reintroduce the missing priority.

**Stable diversity and endogenous alternation.** For $m\ge1$, every interior deterministic trajectory approaches balanced staffing if $(m-1)(1-2\mu)\le1$. Above that threshold, every initially unbalanced trajectory approaches the same pair of alternating shares, up to phase. The outside demand and copying error stay constant.

*Proof.* In log odds $l$ the map is $-h((m-1)l)$, where $h(z)=2\,\mathrm{atanh}((1-2\mu)\tanh(z/2))$. With $a=1-2\mu$ and $t=\tanh(z/2)$ its derivative is $a(1-t^2)/(1-a^2t^2)$. Thus $h$ is odd, bounded, increasing and strictly concave on $z>0$. If the slope at zero is at most one, iteration of the absolute log odds decreases to zero. If it exceeds one, strict concavity and boundedness give exactly one positive fixed magnitude. The increasing magnitude map takes every positive starting point monotonically toward it, while the sign changes each generation. At $m=1$ all starting points reach balance in one step.

With three independent referrals, $z=2x-1$ obeys $z'=-2(1-2\mu)z/(1+z^2)$. At $\mu=3/16$ the attracting shares are $1/4$ and $3/4$. If there is one unit of labor per unit of total demand and each worker can serve only their own job, the fraction of demand served is $1-|x-1/2|$. It averages **75% on the cycle** and **100% at balance**, although the long-run mean share in the first job is $1/2$ in both. By contrast, at $N=8$ a triple of referrals agrees on one mentor with probability $5/144$ at either cycle share, above $1/64$ at balance. An organization rewarded solely for agreements can register an improvement while leaving more requests unanswered.

## When the environment follows the population

Requests can respond to yesterday’s workers. Suppose the odds of requesting the first service equal its provider odds raised to $\beta\ge0$. This changes the demand in the referral experiment, leaving the outside evaluator free to measure service to fixed original needs. Pre-error mentor odds are supply odds to the power $k=1-m+m\beta$. The same proof, now with signed update $h(kl)$, gives three regimes. If $a|k|\le1$ both starting majorities return to balance. For $ak>1$ there are two attracting stationary majorities, with the initial majority determining which priority persists. For $ak<-1$ both priorities persist in an alternating shortage. With $m=3,\mu=3/16$, keeping the requests fixed gives the $1/4,3/4$ cycle. Letting request odds respond with $\beta=4/3$ instead makes the very same two proportions *stable conventions in different communities*. This is selection on what people come to request, which can be measured separately from the capacity to supply both original needs.

Endogenous demand and inheritance have distinct effects. As a perturbation, suppose a mentor acts on its encoded ranking with probability $(1+\kappa)/2$ and does the other job otherwise. An apprentice can copy either the actual ranking or only the observed job. At balanced fixed demand, the derivative of the next ranking share with respect to the current ranking share at $1/2$ is $a(1-m\kappa^2)$ for transmitted rankings. To see this, a shift in the type share changes job supply by $\kappa$ times as much. The log ratio of per-provider nomination weights changes by $-4m\kappa$ times the original shift. The two types have overlapping job choices, giving a second factor of $\kappa$ in their relative return. The prior log odds contribute $4$; conversion back to a share contributes $1/4$, and mutation contributes $a$. Copying the observed job gives slope $a(1-m)\kappa$ instead by substitution in the service-share map. For $m=3,\mu=3/16,\kappa=0.85$, direct transmission returns locally to balance while action copying locally amplifies alternation. An observation of what mentors *do* needs a transmission experiment to establish what their apprentices have inherited.

**The price of acquiring agreement matters.** Let the apprentice try at most $B$ independent referral groups and copy a uniformly drawn existing mentor if all fail. At balance the agreement probability per attempt is $Z=N^{1-m}$, so $A=1-(1-Z)^B$ is the success probability. With fixed demand the derivative of the next share at balance becomes $a(1-mA)$. The derivative of $A$ drops out there because both success and fallback yield the same share. With $N=8,m=3,\mu=3/16$, at least **128** attempts per place are needed for this local instability. At $N=64$ it takes **8,253**. Repeated referrals can produce the strong feedback in the first result, but its physical cost depends sharply on classroom size and the fallback. These thresholds concern local response; they make no assertion about all large perturbations.

## Which process is doing the learning?

The same relative success $r$ can update a transmitted population and the competing dispositions *within* a person. Let learner $i$ begin with propensity $p_i$ for the first action and let weights $w_i$ sum to one. Given full feedback proportional to $(r,1)$, a normalized multiplicative update sends $p$ to $g(p)=rp/[1+(r-1)p]$. Pooling dispositions before selection gives $g(\bar p)$ with $\bar p=\sum_iw_i p_i$. Updating each person and averaging gives a different answer measured by

$$
g(\bar p)-\sum_i w_i g(p_i)
=\frac{r(r-1)}{\bar d^2}\sum_i\frac{w_i(p_i-\bar p)^2}{d_i},
\qquad d_i=1+(r-1)p_i,\quad \bar d=\sum_i w_i d_i.
$$

For $r\ne1$ substitute $g(p)=r(1-1/d)/(r-1)$ and expand $1/d_i$ about $1/\bar d$ with exact quadratic remainder. At $r=1$ both sides vanish. Equal weights on $.1$ and $.9$, with $r=3$, give $17/28$ under local updating and $3/4$ under pooled copying. If learners themselves acquire carrier weights $w_i d_i/\bar d$, their updated average equals $g(\bar p)$ exactly. A common symmetric probability of revision after either update scales the discrepancy by $1-2\mu$.

This specifies a bridge to inheritance by differential reproduction, or to cultural transmission through successful teachers. The material that must be transmitted is the ranking or a procedure that reconstructs it. Within-person learning also requires an action decoder and the assumed feedback. A common average trajectory alone cannot identify those mechanisms. Tracking the same workers before and after feedback, randomizing teachers independently of parentage, and counting transmission weights distinguish causal predictions under these models.

## A measured transmission chain

Sasaki and Biro compared homing routes of birds flying alone, in fixed pairs, or in pairs with one experienced member and a succession of newcomers. Later transmission-chain pairs flew more efficient routes than the controls. Valentini and coauthors used the same experiment to measure in each direction how much the partner’s past turns predicted a bird’s next turn beyond its own history. This comparison lets us ask whether the role “experienced demonstrator” alone predicts the direction of informational influence. [Original experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms15049), [turn-by-turn analysis](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.68653).

I reanalyzed the authors’ archived transfer-entropy estimates at their setting chosen for maximum total information transfer. Novice-to-experienced transfer exceeded the reverse on **185 of 343 flights**. Giving equal weight to each of the ten original chains, five had a larger novice-to-experienced mean. The mean across chains was 0.11950 bits for experienced-to-novice and 0.11973 in the reverse direction. At the authors’ other reported setting, 184 flights and six chain means favored the reverse direction. The 38 pairs change membership over generations; only eight original chains supplied fifth-generation observations. Counting each flight as an independent lineage would give a misleading precision.

In the authors’ surrogate pairings, the mean estimates were 0.11036 and 0.11111, compared with 0.11929 and 0.11992 when averaging the actual flights directly. Both series carry predictive information that is stronger in true pairs. The figures describe movement coordination in these sampled flights. Distinguishing a change of preference from response to a shared cue or a partner requires an additional behavioral intervention. No biological inheritance parameter is inferred. The small reanalysis reads the archived statistics and reconstructs chain membership. It does not refit the GPS models. [Data and code, Valentini et al.](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.14043362), [our script](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/transmission_data.py).

## Evidence and comparisons

With finite copying and positive error all population counts communicate, so the stationary distribution is unique. Exchange of job names makes its mean share exactly one half. For 64 training places, one, two and three referrals give stationary demand coverage of **95.03%, 93.64% and 77.91%**. The respective lag-one correlations of the share are approximately **0, −0.625 and −0.967**. These are numerical stationary calculations from the finite transition matrix. At eight places the checker also enumerates actual referral tuples independently. [Executable](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/service.py).

Environmental feedback, coexisting types and cycling have established accounts in evolutionary games. Chotibut and coauthors already show how equilibrium time-average loads can conceal poor service under multiplicative learning in a routing game. Here repeated client referrals implement the feedback strength, and the carrier-weight calculation tells when interpreting it as individual learning succeeds. [Tilman, Plotkin and Akçay](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14531-6), [Chotibut et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02486), [Harper on replicator weighting and inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1763).
