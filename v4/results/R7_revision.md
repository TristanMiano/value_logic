# R7.2 · Which rules for revising a value can spread?

Paying for evidence before changing a priority can do two things. It helps a recipient decide which changes to make, and it changes which proposals are worth submitting. A population can sustain reliable revision once common even when a lone reliable evaluator loses resources. One way for a new standard to spread is to change what applicants know before they file. In the last construction a single announced trial of mixed review repays its cost with nine later jobs under average returns and ten when the same habitat affects all schools in a generation. The difference comes from the resources left to train successors.

Codex (GPT-6), P4-R7-C001. C5 concerns evaluation of one's own values. In this game an installed ranking determines the job subsequently chosen. A second rule ranks the two available successor procedures (retain or replace the current ranking). An independent external return pays for transmission. A zero internal score difference can be resolved by a declared lottery.

## Rules create their own supply of evidence

Each client starts a new episode with yesterday's job preference. With probability $`p`$ the environment has changed. Installing the opposite ranking then yields real gain $`v`$ relative to retaining yesterday's assignment; installing it unnecessarily incurs loss $`l`$. Retention has gain zero. One adviser observes the need and can file a proposal at a private resource cost $`H`$. It earns one unit only if the client adopts. The same cost is paid for a useful or a useless proposal. Episodes are independently distributed among clients. Add a common positive base endowment for reproduction.

An open rule always values acting on the proposal, a rigid rule retains its ranking, and a guarded rule ranks a replacement above retention exactly when a separate certificate passes. A guard pays $`k`$ per episode to maintain the facility. Its pass rates are $`s`$ when appropriate and $`f`$ when mistaken. The rule is disclosed to the adviser with probability $`q`$. Otherwise the adviser knows only the distribution of rules. A proposal is worthwhile whenever its chance of acceptance exceeds $`H`$. Write $`F`$ for the cost distribution function and take it continuous. All signals, payoffs and filing opportunities used below have now been specified. A physical gate or fixed policy with the same responses, costs and visibility would get the same copying payoff. The score over two successor rankings supplies one represented implementation.

If rule $`j`$ accepts filed proposals with conditional probabilities $`(t_j,u_j)`$ in useful and mistaken cases, let $`(\bar t,\bar u)`$ be their population averages. Its net copying return is

```math
W_j=p v t_j[qF(t_j)+(1-q)F(\bar t)]
 -(1-p)l u_j[qF(u_j)+(1-q)F(\bar u)]-k_j .
```

This follows by separating true from mistaken revisions and disclosed from pooled matches. An independent credential helps when it makes a useful proposal worth filing while making an inappropriate one unprofitable. Any blind admission lottery with $`t_j=u_j`$ elicits the same known-type filing rate in both states.

**A viable meta-value can face a barrier to acquisition.** Consider just guards and rigid rules. Let $`g`$ be the fraction guarded, and suppose $`f`$ lies below all filing costs and $`s`$ above all of them. The guard's advantage is $`psv[q+(1-q)F(sg)]-k`$. It increases with coverage. If $`qpsv<k<psv`$, the guard loses when rare but wins when common. Within the strictly increasing part of $`F`$ the separating initial share is
```math
g_c=F^{-1}\big((k/(psv)-q)/(1-q)\big)/s .
```
Under payoff-proportional copying the share moves in the direction of that advantage. Both extremes attract in this two-rule contest. Charging review mainly when used can remove the barrier. With upkeep $`k_0`$ and an additional fee $`k_1`$ per actual review, a rare disclosed guard at an otherwise rigid population gains $`qp(sv-k_1)-k_0`$. A verifiable credential that raises its disclosure probability can be worth buying out of the additional useful opportunities.

For $`p=1/4,v=3,l=2,s=.9,f=.1,k=.15`$ and $`H`$ uniform on $`[.45,.65]`$, take $`q=.18`$. A guard among rigid clients loses **0.0285** above the common baseline, while a guard among guards gains **0.525**. The initial guarded share must exceed **51.14%** to grow in the deterministic two-rule model. An open mutant at either extreme also loses relative to the resident, earning $`-.135`$ or $`.480`$ respectively. A rare guard could instead pay $`.20`$ for verifiable disclosure and net $`.325`$, above the rigid baseline. If change is rare ($`p=.01`$) and rules are visible, the open, guarded and rigid gains are $`-1.95,-.123,0`$. Guarding pays for itself when the information can improve enough real decisions.

The acquisition threshold is a direction of expected selection. For finite populations we also computed the probability a newly introduced rule supplies *all* descendants. In a birth/death model choose a parent proportional to $`3+W_j`$ and replace a uniformly sampled adult. With 1,024 adults, one guardian and all other adults rigid, eventual guarded fixation has probability **0.00465% at $`q=.18`$**, rising to **1.720% at $`q=.30`$**. If the initial guarded share is one quarter, the corresponding probabilities are 5.10% and 98.83%. Indeed, for current count $`i`$, down/up transition odds equal $`r_i=3/(3+W_G(i/N))`$. Solving the absorption recurrence by successive differences gives fixation from $`j`$ as the partial sum through $`j-1`$ of $`\prod_{i=1}^k r_i`$ divided by the same sum through $`N-1`$, with empty product one. This finite model assumes per-parent weights reflect mean returns across episodes.

## Openness and trustworthy reports can coexist

In an open/guarded population all appropriate proposals are filed. Put $`x`$ for the open fraction and $`y=F(f+(1-f)x)`$ for the hidden-client false filing rate. Open's advantage over guarded is
```math
p(1-s)v+k-(1-p)l[q+(1-q)(1-f)y].
```
It declines with $`x`$. With the numbers above and hidden rules ($`q=0`$), the unique interior rest point is $`x=23/54`$, at which $`y=1/6`$. Both rules earn $`.5`$ above baseline. Asynchronous copying proportional to payoff differences converges to this rest point from any interior two-rule mix, since the advantage is positive below it and negative above. At full disclosure a blind lottery cannot exceed zero net gain here because $`pv-(1-p)l<0`$. The named guard earns $`.525`$. A rule honoring 38% of *failed* certificates as well as all passes still discourages false filings ($`u=.442<.45`$), admits more useful changes and earns $`.5535`$. The three named rules expose important regimes; a wider menu can reward more selective leniency.

What if reporting standards are themselves transmitted? Keep clients hidden and take a constant filing cost $`.55`$. One adviser type files only when the change is useful; another files every time. Both can reproduce according to actual earnings. With $`x`$ still the open fraction and $`y`$ now the fraction of indiscriminate advisers, the two-population copying dynamics are
```math
\dot x=x(1-x)c(y_*-y),\qquad
\dot y=y(1-y)d(x-x_*).
```
Here $`c=(1-p)l(1-f)`$, $`d=(1-p)(1-f)`$, $`x_*=(.55-f)/(1-f)`$, and $`y_*=[p(1-s)v+k]/c`$. The first difference is the return to accepting every proposal. The second is the gain from the additional false filings. When both thresholds lie in the interior, the two standards can cycle indefinitely. Writing $`D(t\Vert z)`$ for binary relative entropy, $`dD(x_*\Vert x)+cD(y_*\Vert y)`$ is conserved. Differentiation cancels the two cross terms. Its nonminimal contours are closed curves in the interior, and the dynamics have nonzero speed along them. The shares have time averages $`x_*,y_*`$ on each orbit by integrating the two log-odds derivatives over a period.

Allow independent positive conversion rates $`m_x^+,m_x^-`$ between the receiver types and likewise for advisers. The resulting extra terms in their log-odds derivatives are $`A_x(x)=m_x^+/x-m_x^-/(1-x)`$ and $`A_y(y)`$ defined likewise. Each decreases from positive to negative infinity. The receiver nullcline $`y=y_*+A_x(x)/c`$ decreases and the adviser nullcline $`x=x_*-A_y(y)/d`$ increases, giving a unique interior intersection $`(x^\dagger,y^\dagger)`$. Using that intersection in the same entropy function gives derivative
```math
d(x-x^\dagger)[A_x(x)-A_x(x^\dagger)]
+c(y-y^\dagger)[A_y(y)-A_y(y^\dagger)].
```
This is strictly negative off the intersection. Persistent variation stabilizes the informative mix in this version. Immediate strategic responses and inherited reporting standards therefore make different predictions about the path, even when the same observations and incentives are available.

**The same useful judgment can fail financially when opportunities move together.** Keep $`p=1/4,s=.9,v=3`$, disclose the guard, raise upkeep to $`.60`$, and endow each type with three units per season. A rigid school has growth factor 3. A guarded school has factor 2.4 in an unchanged market and 5.1 when a change is needed, averaging over many clients' certificates within each market. Its arithmetic mean is 3.075. Now make conditions independent between seasons but shared within each of $`n`$ markets. The school spreads its program equally over those markets and pools proceeds before the next generation. Its almost-sure log growth advantage is
```math
\mathbb{E}\log[(2.4+2.7K/n)/3],\qquad K\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,1/4).
```
It is negative for one or two markets and positive for **three** (−.03470, −.00868, +.00168). The values increase with diversification: each $`(n+1)`$-market average is the average of its $`n`$-market leave-one-out averages, so concavity of the logarithm applies. This is a concrete financing condition for reliable review. Selection acts on funds available to *continue* the rule. [W6 on the two growth criteria](R6_changing_goals.md).

## Which standard survives all rules built from the same certificate?

Allow any two probabilities for honoring a passed or failed certificate, and any blind acceptance lottery without upkeep. At a given false acceptance $`u`$, useful acceptance $`t`$ is largest if the receiver admits passes first. Its upper boundary is $`t=9u`$ up to $`u=.1`$, then $`t=(8+u)/9`$. At full disclosure the expected gross payoff is $`.75tF(t)-1.5uF(u)`$. It increases up to $`u=.45`$ because false advisers still stay out. Beyond $`.45`$ the false-filing term rises faster than the useful gain, both on the quadratic filing interval and on the upper linear interval. The optimum **accepts all passes and $`7/18`$ of failures**, earning $`133/240`$ after upkeep. Its tolerance of imperfect evidence attracts useful revisions without inducing a false applicant at the lowest private cost.

The optimum also gives exact visibility thresholds with a hidden pool. A rare screened rule among rigid residents receives filings only when observed. Its best gain is $`q(169/240)-3/20`$, turning positive at **$`q>36/169`$**. Among residents using the optimized guard, every hidden true proposal files and every hidden false proposal stays out. A fully open blind mutant earns $`.75(1-2q)`$. Blind acceptance up to $`.65`$ earns at most $`.75\times.65`$, less than the incumbent. Above $`.65`$ the blind objective is linear. Thus the optimized guard resists every blind entrant once **$`q\ge47/360`$**. At these $`q`$ it also maximizes screened payoffs: for $`u>.45`$ the marginal false cost is at least $`1.5q`$ on the saturated branch and still higher at the start of the filing interval, whereas the marginal true gain is $`1/12`$. The baseline rare-guard barrier and the maintained review practice therefore overlap in a genuine interval, even against the whole two-probability menu. At zero visibility in the earlier mixed pool, a screened mutant earns $`.75t-.25u-.15`$; the maximum is exactly .5 at the strict guard. A blind mutant cannot improve on the fully open resident's .5. These claims compare invasion earnings; a neutral entrant can still drift. [Exact piecewise-quadratic maximization](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/advisers.py).

## When a standard for revision pays someone else to learn

Remove the assumption that advisers already know whether a proposal is useful. Before learning their filing cost $`H`$, an adviser can buy a private binary indication of symmetric accuracy $`A`$. The receiver's certificate and this indication are independent conditional on usefulness. The adviser gets a unit for adoption. With posterior $`\pi`$ and a publicly guarded recipient, the expected acceptance is $`z=f+(s-f)\pi`$. After seeing $`H`$ the adviser files exactly when $`H<z`$. The adviser's gross return to research is the expected increase in $`(z-H)_+`$, averaging over both the indication and the later cost. Apply the same access rule to a policy whose standard is publicly open or rigid and observing the indication has zero value to this adviser.

For $`A=.8`$ and the original parameters, a good tip arrives with probability $`7/20`$, raises the posterior to $`4/7`$ and induces filing in $`15/28`$ of those cases. The other tip and the uninformed prior induce silence. Buying the indication yields **$`9/896`$** to the adviser before its price and **$`69/560`$** (about .1232) to the guarded recipient after upkeep. Thus a private price .008 funds genuine investigation, even when advisers care solely about adoption. The recipient gains because its certificate is more likely to admit filings warranted by the world; its selective response in turn changes what evidence others buy. The calculation counts the cost draw on both sorts of filings.

There are separate thresholds for activity and useful service. The positive tip first elicits any applications at $`A>.70`$. While its acceptance probability lies between the two cost endpoints, positive recipient return requires $`330A^2-275A+18>0`$, hence $`A>.76173`$ on this branch. Voluntary research additionally requires the actual private price to fall below the adviser's gain. A subsidy can elicit filings before their quality pays for the receiving standard. If the certificate merely copies the same tip, the conditional-independence calculation must be replaced by a joint likelihood. This is a limited construction of reliable second-order judgment through two parties' interests.

**How do these complementary practices originate?** If adviser training has to be bought before clients are known, an untrained population can stay untrained. Let $`x`$ be the guarded share among guarded and rigid recipients, $`y`$ the trained fraction of advisers. Training costs $`d=.008`$ each period and earns $`U=9/896`$ only with a guard. A guard pays $`k=.15`$ and earns $`V=153/560`$ before upkeep only when matched with trained capacity. With independent matching and proportional payoff-difference copying,
```math
\dot x=x(1-x)(Vy-k),\qquad \dot y=y(1-y)(Ux-d).
```
There are two attracting corners and a saddle $`x_*=d/U`$, $`y_*=k/V`$. A joint introduction into a community that previously had neither competence can grow if it starts across the boundary. Once a type is absent, copying alone cannot recreate it. The boundary is the decreasing branch of $`V D(y_*\Vert y)=U D(x_*\Vert x)`$, where $`D`$ is binary relative entropy. Differentiating shows the difference is constant along trajectories. On the branch with one share high and the other low, both move toward the saddle; above the branch both eventually rise and below it both fall. At 10%, 50%, and 95% initial guard participation, the required initial trained fractions are respectively **69.9%, 60.7%, and 49.7%** at these relative copying speeds. Under per-client access advisers can buy the indication after learning they face a guard. Since $`U>d`$, even an isolated guard can then obtain independent information and earn $`V-k>0`$. The changed timing removes the need for a preexisting market of trained partners.

**Publishing a rule and revealing its case decision have different effects.** Suppose an adviser can instead buy advance access to this case's actual certificate. It files for every pass and stays silent on every failure. Its private gross return is $`.3(1-.55)=.135`$, compared with $`9/896`$ for a genuinely independent noisy investigation. Even perfect knowledge of the underlying usefulness, without the certificate, earns only $`.25(.9-.55)=.0875`$. Advisers can want access to the acceptance decision more than accurate knowledge of the work. For the receiving institution the independent route admits $`27/280`$ useful and $`9/1120`$ mistaken changes per opportunity; advance access admits $`9/40`$ and $`3/40`$. With useful reward 3, the latter actually pays *better* when the loss per mistake is less than $`144/25`$ because there are more genuine adoptions. At mistake loss eight the independent route earns **+.075** after upkeep and advance disclosure earns **−.075**. A public standard with the case-specific draw sealed until submission can then pay a real premium for protection. This compares strategic filing with already diagnostic yet redundant information; lowering the error fraction alone cannot rank the two arrangements. Related mechanism-design work specifies costly verification or strategic modification of inputs. [Ben-Porath, Dekel and Lipman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.12.3779), [Hardt et al. (2016)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06980).

## A second pressure on revision without a strategic adviser

A workshop's useful priority is one of two jobs and flips with probability $`0<\nu\le1/2`$ each round, independently of the incumbent. A worker acts on the last installed ranking. A perfect check costs $`C`$ and can install the ranking the current environment supports. Initially suppose the return to the work is observed only in subsequent funding. The incumbent gets no free feedback between checks. Each correct job yields one real unit. Copied programs can be compared by mean work net of check fees across long, independently assigned placements.

If a rule checks every $`L`$ rounds, its net mean work is
```math
J(L)=\frac12+\frac{(1-b^L)/[2(1-b)]-C}{L},\qquad b=1-2\nu .
```
At age $`j`$ since a perfect check, the chance the present state matches the observed state (an even number of changes) is $`(1+b^j)/2`$. Sum from zero through $`L-1`$ and subtract one fee. No check has limiting value $`1/2`$. Some finite interval improves on it exactly when $`C<1/(4\nu)`$. Under the stated observation contract a randomized or history-dependent choice of intervals cannot improve on the best rate. Conditional on the last state and the chosen length, the expected interval return is $`LJ(L)`$. Its age-dependent increments decrease, and the unpaid advantage in an unfinished interval is bounded by the geometric sum. Dividing by total time gives the same upper bound.

Thus very rapid change can select *less* paid scrutiny when an observation expires too soon to earn its price. At $`C=.75,\nu=.05`$ the optimum is a check every seven rounds, giving net mean **.7655**. At $`\nu=.4`$ the same check technology cannot improve the mean .5 of never checking. For another comparison, at $`C=.3,\nu=.1`$ checking every three rounds earns **.8067**. Now let the incumbent see the realized output after acting. In this binary world it learns yesterday's true state for free. Acting on that state already earns .9; advance inspection is worth only .1 each round. The selected rule can keep *revising its ranking from experience* while discontinuing the paid checks. Counting formal reviews alone would misclassify its flexibility. Information value under changing environments and the distinction between observation channels are established themes. [Rivoire and Leibler](https://statbio.net/papers/Rivoire_2011.pdf).

Dekel, Ely and Yilankaya study the evolution of subjective preferences when matches reveal more or less about the other agent, including a broader class of mutants. Dewatripont and Tirole examine incentives to acquire and present information through advocacy. Stabilization by mutation in evolutionary games is established work. The present contribution joins paid, state-sensitive admission to strategic entry, finite access to a self-sustaining review practice, and a comparison with the inheritance of reporting standards. [Dekel, Ely and Yilankaya](https://cet.econ.northwestern.edu/dekel/pdf/evolution%20of-preferences.pdf), [Dewatripont and Tirole](https://doi.org/10.1086/250049), [Bauer, Broom and Alonso](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07839), [W6 commitment game](R6_strategic_values.md), [checker](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/advisers.py).

## What a real review institution makes visible

Registered Reports move a journal's in-principle acceptance decision to before the study results are known, subject to the journal's protocol conditions. Scheel, Schijen and Lakens compared published first hypotheses under this arrangement and conventional publication. From the authors' 243 archived records, their inclusion flag retains 223. Reconstructing their binary coding gives full or partial support in **146/152 conventional reports and 31/71 Registered Reports**. Among hypotheses outside the authors' “close replication” category the figures are **142/148 versus 15/30**. Among close replications they are 4/4 versus 16/41, too few conventional examples even to estimate finite sample odds on this definition. This is a measured association between an institution's review practice and the judgments available to the next investigator. The underlying hypothesis need not be true to receive a supportive report. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467), [public source table](https://github.com/amscheel/positive_result_rates/blob/442ef4c78c921ffa8f5b3ec1d0f2c55a852467be/raw_data/positive_results_in_registered_reports_data.tsv).

What could a population of published reports tell us about external selection for supporting judgments? Let $`p_j`$ be the supportive-result fraction before filtering under arrangement $`j`$, and $`s_{j+},s_{j-}`$ retention probabilities given the two results. Then published odds are $`[p_j/(1-p_j)](s_{j+}/s_{j-})`$. For the non-close-replication subset the observed conventional-to-registered support odds ratio is **$`71/3`$**. Equal differential retention could produce that value if the prefilter odds differed by the entire factor $`71/3`$. If those odds differed by at most fourfold in either direction, the implied conventional-to-registered differential retention ratio would be at least $`71/12`$ using the sample proportions. The bound is a sensitivity calculation; the archive supplies no fourfold limit or count of unsubmitted studies. As a secondary check, counting only *full* support lowers the odds ratio to $`385/57`$ and the corresponding conditional bound to $`385/228`$. Both the rating standard and the selection of questions matter. Recording proposals and all their completed outcomes under a prospectively assigned review rule would separate these channels more directly. [Analysis plan](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/notes.md), [reproduction](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/reports_data.py).

There is also direct evidence about what researchers *judge good* in these arrangements. Soderberg et al. had 353 researchers compare pairs drawn from 29 Registered Reports and 57 comparison papers. On their −4 to +4 scale, estimated differences favoring Registered Reports were .99 for methodological rigor (95% credible interval .62 to 1.35) and .13 for novelty (−.24 to .49). These are assessments of the resulting papers. Field et al. varied what academics were told about preregistration and familiarity when measuring trust. Their manipulation checks excluded 68% of complete datasets, and their Bayes factors left the effects ambiguous. Preregistration and in-principle journal acceptance are different interventions. These studies measure perceived quality and response to a credential; neither directly establishes the accuracy of findings or the reproductive advantage of a review norm. [Soderberg et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01142-4), [Field et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.181351).

### What changes when reviewers see a different finding from the same methods?

Emerson et al. randomly sent reviewers at two journals versions of one fabricated manuscript with the same methods and the same seven identifiable errors. The principal result and its interpretation differed. Of 238 allocated reviewers, 210 returned reviews. For a positive result, 107/110 recommended acceptance and the mean number of errors found was .41. For a no-difference result, 80/100 recommended acceptance and the mean was .85. The identical methods received mean scores of 8.24 and 7.53. Recommendations, methods ratings and error detection therefore supply three distinct measures of the **judgment being selected**. The experiment changes what the evaluator sees. Its returns concern these manuscripts and reviewers; final editorial decisions, later replication and transmission of a reviewer's private motive were outside the comparison. [Emerson et al. (2010), trial and table](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/226270).

How valuable would it have been to assign additional review to the positive version? Consider uniform panels of distinct reviewers from those 110 returned reports, pooling whatever errors their reports detected. We have only the published rounded aggregate statistics. Nevertheless, **two positive-version reviews could cover at most .798 distinct errors on average**, below the .85 detected by one no-difference review. For three reports the sharp compatible range is **.849606–1.166579**. Four give **.968028–1.516661**, always exceeding the observed single-review mean. Knowing just that no positive-version reviewer caught as many as four errors would raise the three-review lower bound to .865323. This is an allocation calculation on the observed reviewers. It motivates measuring overlap between reviews, and a separate test of masking results when assessing methods.

For the calculation, let $`k_i`$ be the number of the seven errors detected by reviewer $`i`$ and $`h_j`$ the number who detected error $`j`$. Rounding the means and sample standard deviations leaves $`\sum k_i=45`$ and $`\sum k_i^2=57`$, so there are only six within-review pairs of detections. The second journal accounts for 14 single detections; the first accounts for all six pairs. A panel of size $`m`$ covers an expected $`\sum_j[1-\binom{110-h_j}{m}/\binom{110}{m}]`$ errors, a sum of concave functions of the integer counts. Its maximum uses the most even counts (seven detections on three errors, six on four), attainable by assigning successive distinct errors cyclically to each review. For each feasible histogram of review sizes, its minimum uses nested detection sets. Indeed the largest $`s`$ column counts can sum to at most $`\sum_i\min(k_i,s)`$, and assigning every row its first $`k_i`$ errors attains that bound for every $`s`$. Four row histograms are compatible; evaluating their minima gives the stated ranges. Reviews may be correlated and different errors may have very different difficulty. No independence of their contents was used. [Integer reconstruction and sharp panel bounds](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/review_panels.py).

## How a standard for judging reviewers can arise

A worker receives sixteen comparable opportunities in one local environment. Its certificate recommends which of two new job rankings to install. Acting on a correct ranking earns one unit toward further placements. In half of placements the certificate is correct on 85% of jobs, in half on 35%; the local rate stays fixed within a placement. A separate procedure choosing the ranking without the certificate succeeds with probability .6. Before committing to a review standard, the worker can inspect up to eight independent settled trials from that environment, paying $`c`$ per trial. It can follow the certificate, reverse it, or use the fallback. Reversing is included because a consistently misleading adviser can carry useful information. The model has actual installed choices and resource returns; the quality of the evaluator is learned from *outcomes of its own past judgments*.

After $`i`$ successful and $`j`$ failed trials, the posterior weights on the two habitats are proportional to $`.85^i.15^j`$ and $`.35^i.65^j`$. Write $`q`$ for their posterior mean reliability. The best stop value is $`S=16\max(q,1-q,.6)`$. With trials remaining the optimal value is the larger of $`S`$ and $`qV_{i+1,j}+(1-q)V_{i,j+1}-c`$. Backward induction gives an ordinary Bayesian benchmark for every rule with these observations. At $`c=.25`$ the best fixed practice earns 9.6 units, the best fixed sample size earns 10.388, and a standard deciding whether to continue from its own findings earns **10.5526**. It buys on average 2.93 trials in the reliable habitat and 3.39 in the misleading one. These are expected material returns under independent placements. The evidence-processing rule could be encoded as a table, a belief update or explicit second-order judgments.

There is a route to most of that advantage by advantageous local changes, even starting with a worker who always follows. One independent receipt increases gross work by exactly one unit if the worker follows after a pass and switches to fallback after a failure. Suppose a valid prior receipt is visible as a byproduct on one fifth of placements. A first variant that can use it for upkeep $`.1`$ gains $`.1`$ per placement. A second variant that buys a trial when the byproduct is missing gains another $`.8(1-.25)=.6`$. Without any byproduct, separately installing the interpreter before acquiring observations can cost resources for no return. Once there is an interpreter, the gross value of one more paid judgment is $`1/3`$ after a pass and $`11/50`$ after a failure. Starting from one stopping rule and repeatedly splitting a reachable branch whenever *one* additional trial strictly pays for itself yields **10.4679** without free receipts or interpreter overhead in the benchmark. Each accepted split improves expected copying resources, so the rule can arise incrementally within this mutation family. Strictly improving one-step splits miss some option value of deeper investigation. Types must still appear and be transmitted, and random finite populations can lose them even when advantageous. [Exact policy and mutation checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/calibration.py).

The source of the receipts changes what selection can learn. In the reliable habitat release two fifths of successes and of failures. In the misleading habitat release $`34/35`$ of successes and $`6/65`$ of failures. In **both** habitats each registered trial now yields a public success, public failure or blank with respective probabilities $`.34,.06,.60`$. Even an observer who knows the total number of cases cannot discriminate the two worlds from this record. Direct prospective tests restore the calibration above. Selected evidence can also suffice if its bias is bounded: if the reported success share is $`.85`$ and the success-to-failure retention ratio is at most $`\Gamma`$, then actual reliability is at least $`.85/(.85+.15\Gamma)`$. Below $`\Gamma=34/9`$ it is safe in mean return to follow instead of using the .6 fallback; at a twofold ratio the lower bound is $`17/23`$. Sampling uncertainty would have to enter an actual finite-data guarantee. A meta-value that respects externally validated bounds can therefore use imperfect disclosure without treating review prestige alone as proof of reliability.

**Who funds a judgment that everyone in a team can use?** Each of four workers gets sixteen jobs in the same local habitat. A collector pays 1.2 from its own reproduction receipts to obtain one validation. Everyone can use the evidence. With probability $`a=.7`$ the four workers inherit their funding rule from a common teacher, and otherwise their teachers are drawn independently from the population. Separately, with probability $`\rho`$ the collected receipts are traceably the same case; otherwise they are independent. Evidence access is public before jobs are chosen. Sharing a standard and sharing an observation are distinct design parameters.

Let $`B_m`$ be each worker's mean optimal gross return with $`m`$ independent validations in the model above. For $`m=0,\ldots,4`$ these are 9.6, 10.6, 10.888, 11.0127 and 11.31006. The fourth observation can be more valuable than the third because the joint evidence can move the actual choice across a threshold. Put $`b_0=B_0`$ and $`b_m=(1-\rho)B_m+\rho B_1`$ for $`m\ge1`$. With collector share $`x`$, its advantage in expected copying receipts over a noncollector is
```math
a(b_4-b_0)+(1-a)\mathbb E[b_{M+1}-b_M]-1.2,\qquad M\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(3,x).
```
Condition on common schooling versus independent formation and compare actual public returns net of the individual fee. At $`\rho=0`$ this advantage is positive at every share; at $`\rho=1`$ it is negative at every share. With only 13% common cases it is positive near zero, negative at $`x=.75`$, and positive again near one. The cubic's four Bernstein coefficients have signs $`+,+,-,+`$. Substituting $`x=t/(1+t)`$ and multiplying by $`(1+t)^3`$ gives a polynomial with two sign changes, hence exactly two interior roots given the observed brackets. Under proportional payoff-difference copying the equilibrium at .63858 attracts, and the threshold at .90918 separates its basin from complete collection. Thus the same lineage structure can select funding by everyone, coexistence, or the abandonment of costly scrutiny, depending on whether apparently separate judgments have separate grounding. [Selection and root calculations](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/calibration.py).

## When a judge should change how it tests its own standards

Take the original guarded and open rules. Consider two equally likely communities, constant through a placement. Useful advisers in both have filing costs uniform on $`[.45,.65]`$. In A, mistaken advisers have these costs too. In B their costs exceed one, so filing never repays them. An opportunity and the material effect of each accepted change can be registered even if nobody files. Opportunities are independent, allocated before advisers choose whether to file, and the same adviser cannot move to another trial.

Suppose the institution tries open review on fraction $`\tau`$ of cases, assigning the rule *after* the filing decision. Advisers know the frequency. A mistaken adviser's chance of acceptance is $`u=.1+.9\tau`$, at or below the cheapest cost as long as $`\tau\le7/18`$. Therefore no mistaken adviser in either community files and every useful one files. For every such blinded trial the entire registered record, including silent cases and settled outcomes, has the same law in A and B. Among observed applicants the open rule accepts more good changes and saves upkeep. Yet on *publicly installing* open review, A earns $`-.75`$ per opportunity and B earns $`+.75`$. Both earn $`.525`$ under public guarded review. No number of these after-filing experiments settles which public rule to adopt.

The institution can instead announce one open pilot *before* filing. An inappropriate opportunity (probability $`3/4`$) reveals A by a mistaken change or B by silence; a useful change provides no distinction. Under an equal prior the pilot earns zero in expectation. Compared with continuing guarded review, it sacrifices $`.525`$ now and gains $`(3/8)(.75-.525)`$ per later opportunity by opening in the identified B cases. Seven subsequent opportunities repay it. This is a second-order decision about the process which evaluates a proposed change of standard. A staff member applying an ordinary policy experiment with the same announcement timing can earn the same return.

A stronger competitor keeps the blind lottery permanently. Across the two possible communities its mean return is
```math
.525+.225\tau-.75uF(u),\qquad u=.1+.9\tau,
```
where $`F`$ is the uniform filing-cost distribution function on $`[.45,.65]`$. The linear gain increases up to $`\tau=7/18`$. On the interior of the filing-cost interval the marginal deduction is at least $`.75(.9)(.45/.2)>.225`$, and above the interval it is $`.75(.9)`$. The best fixed lottery therefore earns $`.6125`$ in either community. Comparing the pilot with *this* practice, retaining the lottery except when B is identified, gives a net gain of $`-.6125+H(33/640)`$ for $`H`$ later decisions. **Twelve further decisions** suffice; eleven do not cover this particular pilot. An optimal sequence of several other experiments might pay sooner. Correlated job arrivals, failure to register silence or applicants shifting between arms would require additional evidence. This is a small, solved instance of decisions changing the data on which they will later be judged. [Perdomo et al. (2020)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/perdomo20a.html), [payoff checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/advisers.py).

A less costly experiment works earlier. Publicly commit to a post-filing open draw with probability **$`11/18`$** on the pilot job. The most expensive mistaken adviser in A now expects acceptance at rate $`.65`$, so all file there (endpoint ties have probability zero). Silence identifies B whenever its opportunity is inappropriate, exactly as in the totally open pilot. The pilot itself earns $`.175`$ on average. Against $`.6125`$ the resulting experiment first pays with **nine subsequent jobs**, a gain of $`17/640`$ at nine and a loss of $`1/40`$ at eight.

This cutoff is sharp for one announced mixture followed by the choice between the best standing lottery and public opening. For $`\tau`$ between $`7/18`$ and $`11/18`$ write $`y=F(u)`$. Immediate loss relative to the standing lottery is $`.2875y+.15y^2`$. Only silence can raise the chance of B above its prior. Taking the better continuation after silence yields an expected gain per later job of $`(3/8)\max\{0,(109y-98)/80\}`$. When $`H\ge2`$ the net gain is increasing throughout the informative part where this maximum is positive, so its best value there occurs at $`y=1`$. For $`H=1`$ even the maximum later gain cannot repay the immediate loss on that part. Larger $`\tau`$ produces no additional evidence and lowers the pilot's payoff; smaller ones do best at the standing lottery. The beneficial rule change concerns what contributors *know* about the test before they decide to take part. [Exact mixed-pilot checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/advisers.py).

For the fully open pilot, the time needed for *evolutionary spread* can exceed the time needed to repay the expected fee. Suppose all schools encounter the same independently drawn A or B community each generation. Many placements within it average the proposal and certificate draws. Each earned unit finances another copy and two additional base units are available each generation. With $`H`$ later jobs the blind school's copying factor is $`C=2+(H+1)(.6125)`$. Relative to it, a pilot school loses $`109/80`$ copies in A and gains $`(11/80)(1+3H/4)`$ in B. Its long-run relative log growth is positive exactly when the product of its two copying factors exceeds $`C^2`$. At $`H=12`$ it has positive mean gain $`1/160`$ yet a smaller product. Thirteen later jobs still leave the product below the incumbent's; **fourteen** first put it above. Before twelve even the arithmetic premium is negative. Thereafter the product difference increases, since the relative gains sum to a positive increasing quantity and even the A factor stays positive. The less costly $`11/18`$ pilot first wins the same log-growth test with **ten later jobs** (nine under mean resources). Here the two relative gains are $`-37/40`$ and $`.05+(33/320)H`$; the same product test applies. Paying for a more informative test can thus be favored under either criterion, at different production horizons. The comparison treats a planning rule as a transmitted object with actual accounts, and assumes a new habitat each generation. If instead a workshop stays in one habitat and its successors inherit a verified case record, pilot receipts can outlast the founding generation. With the $`11/18`$ pilot, silence identifies B with probability $`3/4`$ per trial there. In A an accepted mistaken filing identifies A with probability $`(3/4)(.65)`$ per trial. Repeating until either kind of receipt costs finitely many trials almost surely in each habitat (mean at most $`1/[(3/4)(.65)]`$). An institution able to finance that initial search can then use its locally better standing rule indefinitely. Transfer of this judgment to a different habitat reopens the calibration question.

What exactly can selection distinguish? Two architectures with identical laws for public announcements, choices of successor rules, received outcomes, operating costs and faithful copies in every offered environment have identical external growth. Coupling the same proposals and outcomes proves this directly. A rule tested only on today's filings has met a much weaker requirement. Presenting a paid choice between a hidden test and the public pilot above can distinguish a fixed admission routine from a judge that anticipates the change in supply. A compiled policy that also handles that choice correctly belongs to the same behavioral class at this scope. Differences in which future choices are available, or which rule can be learned and copied with fewer resources, still matter. [W6 on reconstructing values from policies](R6_value_identification.md).

**Aim-balance self-review.** The first games risked making reliable review look selectable by supplying informed advisers for free. Charging advisers to investigate, allowing correlated evidence and letting blind lotteries compete located actual advantages for open, guarded and rigid practices. The controlled review study also supplied a measure of what reviewers detected that does not depend on whether an editor liked their recommendation. Announcing a change before filing can pay even when hidden evaluations of the same rule agree indefinitely. These results advance C5 for explicit successor rankings and observed choices of procedure. Whether the same behavior arises through a represented evaluation of one's values or an unrepresented gate requires an intervention on the proposed procedure or access to the implementation. A live comparison could vary announcement timing and follow all opportunities to settlement.
