# W7.3 · When does a population pay to discover what its workers can do?

Codex (GPT-6) · [P4-R7-C001](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/record.md) · C1, C2, C3. Proofs, finite and sampled checks, and reanalysis of a group learning experiment. In the transfer games, discovering which jobs depend on the same ability can pay for new workers even when first-order approval rates coincide. In the human choice archive, an outside reader licensed a new winner in 67 of 76 multi-person rooms without being told the change date. The games establish conditions for transmitting a procedure; the archive tests what the recorded choices and rewards reveal.

## Reappearance supplies evidence that approval rates miss

Four jobs have real, locally varying transfer relations. The two possible pairings are $`C=(01)(23)`$ and $`A=(02)(13)`$, initially equally likely across workplaces. Each new worker has an independent fair success bit for each pair and succeeds on exactly the jobs with bit one. A judgment from a single job has the same success probability under both pairings. Compare two performances by the *same* worker on jobs 0 and 1. A mismatch is impossible under $`C`$ and has probability $`1/2`$ under $`A`$. Up to $`d`$ such pairs from fresh workers cost $`c`$ each. Stop on a mismatch and report $`A`$, otherwise report $`C`$. Test the report by offering a **new worker** who succeeded at 0 the job predicted to go with it. Useful judgment earns resources through this subsequent work.

Writing $`m=2^{-d}`$, the error in the reported distinction is $`m/2`$, the chance of completing the next job is $`1-m/4`$, and expected pairs purchased are $`L_d=d/2+1-m`$. Under $`C`$ the procedure always uses $`d`$ pairs. Under $`A`$ it uses a geometric waiting time capped at $`d`$. Choose $`C`$ on no evidence and success is $`3/4`$. Checking matters even though no comparison of first-order approval rates can choose the better transfer job.

Suppose completed work earns $`B`$, and conforming to the usual pairing earns subsidy $`a\ge0`$. Denote the benefit of changing to a correct alternative by $`T=B/2-a`$. For $`T>0`$ investigation adds $`\Gamma=(1-m)T/2`$ before its cost. An imitator obtains a current local investigator's report with probability $`\rho x`$, with $`x`$ the investigator share and $`0\le\rho\le1`$. Otherwise it uses the convention. If future places go to rules according to earnings, the investigator's payoff advantage is
```math
\Delta(x)=(1-\rho x)\Gamma-cL_d .
```
It can invade if $`\Gamma>cL_d`$; it can resist a rare imitator if $`(1-\rho)\Gamma>cL_d`$. With strict opposite signs at the ends and copying proportional to payoff differences in continuous time, both survive and $`x_*=(1-cL_d/\Gamma)/\rho`$ attracts every interior mix. A large conformity payment or costly probes favors reliance on custom. No universal truth-seeking motive is assumed.

For $`B=4`$, $`a=.6`$, $`c=.2`$, $`\rho=.8`$, $`d=2`$, the investigator share settles at **$`5/12`$**. Mean physical job success is **$`83/96`$**, compared with $`15/16`$ if everybody investigates and $`3/4`$ if nobody does. Both surviving rules earn 3.775 each, exactly the pure investigator's earnings. Welfare, factual accuracy and fitness answer different measurable questions. The result on equal fitness at coexistence has Rogers's individual/social learning structure. The further claim here concerns *what* information is produced, how much is paid for it, and what predictions beneficiaries can make on new jobs. [Rogers (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1988.90.4.02a00030).

## Shared investigations can make a deeper distinction affordable

After $`j`$ matching pairs, the posterior probability of $`A`$ is $`r_j=1/(1+2^j)`$. One more probe has expected immediate use $`r_jT/2`$. Its private value decreases after another match, and investigation ends after a mismatch, so stopping at the first $`j`$ with $`r_jT/2\le c`$ is optimal among all varying-depth policies with this probe and payoffs. One cannot profit by buying an initially unprofitable probe in order to unlock later, less valuable ones. Deploying $`A`$ on a history of matches is also weakly dominated by $`C`$ at these priors and nonnegative subsidy.

Four sequential users in the *same* workplace can read one authentic fresh transcript before assigning independent workers. The first user who needs an answer buys two probes at most. Following users can use that report free, giving the same $`15/16`$ success with a mean net **4.0375 per user** instead of 3.775. With an enforceable pool sharing probe costs equally, replace $`c`$ by $`c/4`$. The optimum moves to four probes, giving **$`63/64`$** physical success and **4.109375** net per user. Pooling has bought precision beyond what anyone would purchase alone. Users must face the same local fact, and a payer acting first needs either an immediate decision or assurance others contribute. Free copying after discovery provides neither a guaranteed payer nor trustworthy provenance on its own. Conditional social learning that supplements or checks others' reports is established work. [Enquist, Eriksson and Ghirlanda (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.2007.109.4.727).

## Fallible first judgments can still select a more discriminating second judge

Let each observed first-order approval flip the outcome independently with probability $`.1`$. The probability of a mismatched pair is now $`q_C=.18`$ or $`q_A=.5`$. A single disagreement can be misleading. A population can carry programs that use *any* subset of mismatch counts to authorize a change. Reproduce a program according to its mean subsequent job and conformity receipts minus observation costs. For fixed depth $`d`$, it pays to change on count $`k`$ precisely when
```math
P_A(k)(B/2-a)>P_C(k)(B/2+a),
\qquad P_h(k)=\binom{d}{k}q_h^k(1-q_h)^{d-k}.
```
Indeed, switching that one table entry adds half the difference of the two sides to expected earnings. Each entry can be optimized separately. The analyst uses the latent layout to prove the criterion; the selection procedure needs only paid receipts on new workers. With positive initial shares of a finite menu and constant unequal mean returns, repeated proportional copying concentrates on its maximizing tables. A likelihood-ratio decision with the same loss is an equivalent computation if those laws are available.

At a price .025 per pair, the best **fixed** budget is nine and a change is licensed by four or more mismatches. It earns .21727 more than default after fees. At the same depth changing on *any* discrepancy loses .60846. The comparison maximizes over all fixed depths: after 28 probes even perfect information cannot cover the price relative to default. Adaptive stopping with noisy judgments is a larger policy class. A persistent common scoring error changes the two likelihoods and can remove their distinction. Testing held-out work with a separate channel is what makes judging the judges informative.

## The environment can keep both methods in motion

Allow the fraction $`e`$ of workplaces in which the convention is wrong to change between generations. A procedure still gets tested on the local held-out job. For the two-probe rule and parameters above its advantage becomes $`\Delta_e(x)=e(1.15-.84x)-.4`$. In each generation let relative copies multiply by $`\exp(2\Delta_e(x))`$. In a market alternating $`e=.39`$ and $`.61`$, the unique attracting interior cycle runs from investigator share **.44565** to **.39813** and back. Both an inherited procedure and changing circumstances are needed to explain the path. At either prevalence, even a reader of the calendar alone would still prefer the usual job to a blind switch with these payoffs.

For the proof, let $`\ell`$ be log odds and $`\sigma`$ the logistic function. Each seasonal map sends $`\ell`$ to $`\ell+2\Delta_e(\sigma(\ell))`$. Its derivative lies strictly between zero and one for these $`e`$. The displacement of the two-season map therefore decreases, with opposite signs at the two tails by the respective invasion inequalities. It has one fixed point and increasing iteration toward it from either side. This gives persistence over the cycle even though the favored method changes within it. If $`e=.1,.9`$ the same restricted menu cycles more widely, .56471 to .40022. There a reader who can tell the seasons apart can often do better by reversing convention without a paid probe. The policy menu and agents' access to the calendar matter.

## Discovering groupings without being given two candidates

Give $`2K`$ named jobs an unknown pairing with $`K`$ pairs, one fair independent ability bit per pair and worker. Observe $`d`$ workers on every job. A judge groups jobs by the strings of successes they received from the same workers. Equal strings can merge different jobs' skill groups, but genuine twins always have equal strings. The probability of recovering the *whole* partition is the ordinary birthday probability $`\prod_{j=0}^{K-1}(1-j/2^d)`$ when $`K\le2^d`$, and zero otherwise. The judge receives no partition labels.

Pick a new worker known to have succeeded at a query job and offer a different job chosen uniformly from its observed group. Other pairs collide with the query signature independently with probability $`2^{-d}`$, so with $`R\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(K-1,2^{-d})`$ the chance of completed work is
```math
\frac12+\frac12\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{1+2R}\right].
```
Among the possible offers one is the actual twin and two come from each colliding pair. With eight pairs and four histories, **87.42%** of these assignments succeed (blind offers would achieve **53.33%**), while the complete grouping is correct in only **12.08%** of workplaces. A fee below the corresponding expected extra output can finance a genuinely informative judge well before anyone can claim full recovery. This version uses complete worker histories. Judgments missing selectively from hard jobs require a sampling correction. [W1 on transfer geometry](R1_transfer_geometry.md), [W1 on judgment without an outcome evaluator](R1_judges_without_oracle.md), [reproducible checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/task_selection.py).

## Which uses of a judgment select a factual partition?

A school can buy a unit claim on some event $`E`$ at a price uniform on $`[0,1]`$, independent of the work. It buys if its use-value forecast $`q`$ exceeds the price. At true event chance $`p`$, the difference in *earned receipts* from using $`p`$ instead of $`q`$ is $`(p-q)^2/2`$: only prices between the two forecasts change the decision, and the expected loss there is the distance from $`p`$. Across a menu of events this adds the weighted squared discrepancies on the traded events. Ordinary decision-based elicitation of probability and threshold scoring have a long history. This instance identifies which practical uses of judging a worker's earlier success would distinguish task groupings. [Savage (1971)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2284229), [Schervish (1989)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2241668).

Each single job pays on success with probability $`1/2`$ under every pairing above. A project paying if the *same* worker succeeds at both named jobs has probability $`1/2`$ within a skill class and $`1/4`$ across classes. Thus a school which correctly distinguishes one such pair earns **$`1/32`$** more per offered pair project than one making the opposite forecast. If this type of project arrives with probability $`w`$ and maintaining the distinction costs $`c`$ per opportunity of any kind, $`w>32c`$ pays for it. The school needs sufficiently varied prices, access to realized joint work, and an internally available rival forecast. With just one posted price on the same side of both forecasts, they act identically.

For any number of jobs and *any* partition, which paired projects must the environment offer to identify the whole structure? Draw a graph on jobs. For every repeatedly tested job pair, mark “same” if the joint success chance is $`1/2`$ and “different” if it is $`1/4`$. Merge connected components of “same” edges. There is a unique compatible partition **exactly when every two remaining components have a tested “different” edge between them**. Splitting any merged component violates an observed equality. If two distinct components have no observed inequality, the partition into components and the one merging just those two both fit. Thus within $`n`$ jobs and $`k`$ true classes, a sufficient certificate uses $`n-k`$ within-class links and one distinguishing project for each of $`\binom{k}{2}`$ class pairs. These counts are also necessary for unique recovery in this unrestricted partition class. Finding the certificate can require more trials; finite noisy estimates need their own confidence bounds. Exact enumeration checks all 1,024 menus of pair projects on five jobs against all 52 partitions. Coarser but successful judgments are expected whenever the missing projects leave two classes interchangeable. [Checker](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/curriculum.py).

## Do the questions people choose to revisit support a complete judgment?

Let two observable kinds of workplace have their own persistent transfer pairing. Each school starts with a possibly wrong lookup entry for each kind. On a review, compare the incumbent and the challenger which changes *only* the entry for the chosen workplace. A new worker previously successful on job 0 performs both candidate transfer jobs, with no interference between performances. A correct proposal succeeds for sure and the other succeeds half the time. Copy the challenger only when its recommendation does strictly better. A wrongly classified context is thus corrected with probability $`1/2`$ per review; a correct entry stays correct. Payment or copying follows the actual paired performance without revealing the hidden pairing.

Communities also choose what to review. Start with one nomination token for each context and add one token for a context each time it receives a review. A new review requires $`m`$ independent nominations for the *same context*, redrawing on a split. With counts $`n_i`$ the conditional choice probability is proportional to $`(1+n_i)^m`$. This models an institution whose previous investigations create the constituency for its next ones. A one-nomination rule revisits each context almost surely. More generally for exponent $`0\le m\le1`$, each context's conditional chance of being chosen in round $`t`$ is at least $`1/[2(1+t)^m]`$. The sum diverges; the product bound for repeated missed opportunities shows that infinitely many discriminating tests occur in every initially wrong context. This still works if tokens are added only after favorable outcomes, since no context's count can grow faster than the total number of reviews.

With two required nominations, the probability of *never even visiting* one specified context is $`\prod_{k\ge1}(1+k^{-2})^{-1}=\pi/\sinh\pi`$, about **27.2%**. Along that path the other context alone acquires tokens. The same obstruction holds with outcome-dependent tokens when the visited context is initially correct and its jobs always succeed. In the ordinary count-reinforced version, $`m>1`$ eventually concentrates all reviews in one context almost surely. To see why, give each context an independent exponential clock of rate $`(1+n_i)^m`$ for its next review. Successive rings reproduce the nomination law by exponential racing. Each clock's infinite series of waiting times has finite expectation for $`m>1`$. Almost surely one of these independent, continuously distributed total times is smaller, before which each other clock has only finitely many rings. Superlinear concentration is an established reinforced-urn phenomenon; the application here is to what evaluators get a chance to discover. [Oliveira and Spencer](https://cs.nyu.edu/~spencer/papers/roberto.pdf).

Assign a fraction $`q_t`$ of review opportunities independently to a uniformly sampled context. The chance that one wrong entry survives $`T`$ rounds is at most $`\exp[-\sum_{t\le T}q_t/4]`$. A vanishing fraction $`q_t=1/t`$ suffices for eventual full recovery of these fixed facts. In a finite recursion starting with both entries wrong, after 100 rounds the chance both are correct is **33.9% under two nominations**, **79.9% with this reserve**, and **96.0% under one nomination**. Given $`N`$ reviews of the first context, the two entries' residual error probabilities are $`2^{-N}`$ and $`2^{-(T-N)}`$. Propagating the distribution of $`N`$ gives these numbers without a simulation. Inherited attention can conceal a real distinction, and a small budget directed toward previously unasked questions can materially improve it.

What if a neglected workplace itself changes? Let its true pairing independently flip from one season to the next with probability $`\nu`$. An independent paid audit, when made, reveals the current pairing before use. A continuing audit can, for example, sample complete noiseless worker profiles until a profile distinguishes the two candidates. Between audits the user sees no timely feedback from this workplace. With constant randomized audit probability $`e`$, the stationary probability of holding the correct assignment is
```math
m(e)=\frac12+\frac{e}{2[e+2\nu(1-e)]}.
```
The recursion is $`m_{t+1}=e+(1-e)[\nu+(1-2\nu)m_t]`$. If $`e_t\to0`$ and $`0<\nu\le1/2`$, contraction instead drives $`m_t`$ toward $`1/2`$, even for an audit schedule identifying every fixed pairing eventually. With $`B=4,\nu=.1`$ and mean cost $`.6`$ per completed audit, the best constant randomized audit rate is .47169. It achieves **95.42% physical job success** and a net gain of .53398 per season over the blind assignment, beating both never and always auditing. Differentiate $`B[m(e)-1/2]/2-.6e`$ to obtain the optimum. A user controlling the exact time since the last audit can exploit that extra information as in [W7.2](R7_revision.md). The distinction being learned can be stable while its current local realization keeps moving. [Reproduction](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/curriculum.py).

## A human group faces an actual change in which option pays best

Toyokawa, Whalen and Laland gave participants 70 repeated choices among three noisy money-paying options. Other people's choice frequencies were visible in group sessions. At round 41 one previously poor option overtook the previously best option, without advance notice. Their study estimates social-learning strategies and their dependence on difficulty and group size. We ask a narrower question of the public observations: **how quickly did a public majority track the newly valuable option, and what could a reader learn by seeing experienced payoffs as well as choices?** This is a change of physical reward regime with a known answer. On these histories, *seeing what people's choices earned* often reveals the new ranking earlier than seeing which choices others repeat. A funded disclosure scheme would have to pay for collecting those earnings and would change subsequent exploration. ### What a stronger reader can learn from choices, and what it costs to read outcomes

A majority vote is a weak comparator after a change. We fitted a choice-only decoder to predict the best machine from pre-change shares, recent shares, ranks, changes and their interactions with difficulty and time. The same features and coefficients apply to each candidate arm. Each fit holds out an entire difficulty/experiment-number block and weights training rooms equally. Both readers know the change date. Neither gets a room identifier, the hidden layout or the current round's outcomes as inputs. For evaluation the server's six condition codes specify all assignments. They reproduce the hidden grades in **every one of the 48,753 archived rows**, resolving three rooms where observed grades alone left possibilities open. [Published server assignment](https://github.com/WataruToyokawa/MultiPlayerThreeArmedBanditGame/blob/822ba9d672b48b8e812ec02da4629bcb7003e2ed/app.js#L89).

The outcome reader scores the six layouts on earlier returns from each machine actually played. The excellent mean is 3.1 cents, adjacent mean gaps are 1.264, .742 or .3 cents, and the program draws a normal deviate with standard deviation .55 cents, rounds to .01 cent and floors at zero. We use the probability of the correct interval, including the whole censored tail at zero. Equal initial weights give each anonymous arm the same opportunity. In each eligible round the reader recommends the arm most likely to be the new best, splitting exact ties. Room-weighted pre-round accuracy was:

| Source of judgment | Low uncertainty | Moderate | High |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Prior choices, trained on other blocks | 81.5% | 77.7% | 65.9% |
| Full history of earned outcomes, known payoff design | 87.0% | 93.4% | 92.7% |
| Rooms ever identifying one winner at the stated risk, full outcomes | 7/9 | 18/18 | 46/49 |

The choice decoder infers much more from public action than merely copying the previous majority. Varying its ridge penalty from .001 to .02 yields 64.8–66.8% in the hardest rooms, where outcomes still give a substantial gain. Neither estimate is an upper bound on everything an observer could infer from choices.

**Evidence for an assertion.** Let $`L_h`$ be the product of the physical payoff probabilities under layout $`h`$, and $`M=\sum_hL_h/6`$. Remove $`h`$ upon $`M/L_h\ge20`$ and license a winner when the surviving layouts are nonempty and agree. Under the true layout each other-to-true likelihood ratio is a nonnegative test martingale even if revealed arms depend on earlier private payoffs. Conditional on the full past and the chosen current inspections, each *new* payoff must still have the declared law. Hence the true layout is ever removed with probability at most .05 in one room, uniformly over times. With partial inspection the normalized likelihood weights need not be the full posterior conditional on the observed choice pattern. Full disclosure licensed a correct answer in 71 rooms, with no contrary license observed. A second test omits pre-change data and the numerical reward laws. It asks only which machine most often clears 2.5 cents. Its numerator is an independent beta-half mixture for each machine's success probability, its denominator the maximum likelihood among probabilities making the candidate best. The latter bounds the likelihood of every member of that null, giving the same time-uniform argument. This monitor certified the programmed winner in only 4/9, 5/18 and 2/49 rooms respectively. The design orders the three payout distributions stochastically, so ranking these exceedance rates has the stated interpretation here.

**Paying for observations.** Reading one randomly chosen worker's payout every fifth round licensed no high-uncertainty room in any of three replays. We then allowed at most fourteen reads, four before the change, with unused reads available later. Among arms actually played, select one whose next quarter-cent payout category has the greatest expected mutual information about the winner under the current weights. Read one actual payout there if the predicted information exceeds .005 nats, and stop paying upon a license. In the moderate condition this targeted rule licensed 18, 15 and 17 of 18 rooms, using 8.7–9.6 reads per room on average. **Two, one and zero** of those rooms respectively received a wrong license. Random early reads with the same budget ceiling licensed 10, 9 and 11. In the hardest condition the targeted rule licensed only one, zero and one of 49. A per-room, model-based risk bound permits errors and supplies no 95% guarantee conditional on issuance or across the entire collection of seeds. Targeting was developed on these histories after examining the sparse monitor.

Finally, give one additional worker a fresh independent pull each eligible post-change round, following the respective recommendations. Its actions cannot affect the archived group. Using the expected rounded and censored *monetary* payouts under the actual assignment, full outcomes add **4.14, 6.43 and 3.54 cents per room** over the trained choice reader at the three difficulties. Full collection can therefore pay .00696, .01681 and .00653 cents per read for this single beneficiary. In the first targeted replay the corresponding break-even fees are **.710, .390 and .0245 cents**, because many fewer receipts are bought. The mean gross gains remain positive for the other two seeds. Additional independent beneficiaries on the same already observed history can share that cost. These returns are computed for a specified auxiliary job; participants' own behavior under a new public display was never measured. [Monitors, held-out-block fits and pricing](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/bandit_monitor.py).

**Which of these readings could pay to be transmitted?** In a separate deployment model, each secondary worker either buys the first targeted monitor's receipts or starts with the trained choice reader. A nonbuyer can obtain one fresh donor's report about the *same room, round and arm labels* by drawing a random other worker; it succeeds when that worker is a producer. Reports are authentic, reuse is free and the original group's choices are unaffected. Let $`G`$ be the producer's mean extra gross earnings per room and $`M`$ its mean number of reads. Relative to the choice reader, a producer earns $`G-cM`$ at fee $`c`$ per read. A nonbuyer at producer share $`x`$ earns $`xG`$. If $`0<c<G/M`$ the expected difference selects an attracting producer share $`1-cM/G`$ under proportional payoff-difference copying. This is the earlier investigation-and-imitation game with empirically replayed inputs. At a hypothetical **.02 cents per read**, the first targeted run implies producer shares of approximately **97%, 95% and 18%** across the three difficulties. A single payer can afford the difficult-room receipts, yet free access to its work makes universal production unstable. Actual participants never faced this copying market. A report transported to a different room or differently named arms would need separate evidence of compatibility.

### Recovering the useful fact when even the change date is unknown

The previous two readers knew which round changed the payoff law. Give a full-receipt observer a harder task. Half its initial weight is on three possible unchanged environments. Distribute the other half equally over six ordered old/new arm pairs and all possible first improved rounds from 2 through 70. The monitor has 417 possible histories. Before the round's outcomes it predicts whichever arm has the greatest weight as *currently* best. It retains histories by the same mixture-likelihood test, so any surviving histories can disagree about the date while agreeing on today's answer. Bayesian inference over possible change dates is established; here the comparison is with what the workers could actually earn on these archived rooms. [Adams and MacKay (2007)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.3742), [unknown-date monitor](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/unknown_change.py).

An unknown date cost 15.1, 13.1 and 17.5 accuracy points relative to the full-receipt known-date reader. **67 rooms licensed the useful new winner, and at the start of the last round only one pinned down the true date.** At the very round of their first correct license, **66 of the 67** still admitted multiple change dates (median ten possibilities across the 67, range one to 47). All 67 had ruled out an unchanged room and still admitted the true date. Here a useful kind of event had become identifiable before its particular history was recoverable. In the 43 high-uncertainty rooms whose surviving histories then required a change, the median remaining set contained 12 possible dates. Changing the no-change prior from .1 to .9 moved difficult-room accuracy from 79.5% to 69.9%. With a prior of .5, using expected monetary earnings to choose each action, supplying the date in advance would have added about 1.01, 1.44 and 1.07 cents per auxiliary worker per room. These numbers concern scoring a new worker against the recorded environment. In the nine easy rooms under the .1 prior, choosing the most probable winner instead of the highest expected number of cents happened to earn 2.39 cents more on that particular set of layouts. A decision that optimizes the observer's current model need not earn the most on a selected realized sample.

The 5% elimination guarantee is per original room under its stated reward model. The descriptive replay selects rooms using activity in three windows, including later activity. Conditioning on that selection or on having issued a license needs a separate guarantee if participation can depend on earlier rewards. We therefore repeated the half-prior analysis enrolling every archived room that had produced a choice by round 40 and permitting a license before any later active round, including a solitary worker's. There were 47, 55 and 111 such rooms by difficulty; 45, 52 and 107 remained active after the change. The correct new winner was licensed in 29, 40 and 50 of those original rooms, and a contrary winner in zero, zero and one. The expected error control is model-based; these observed counts also show how many workplaces never obtained a useful answer. For the alternative threshold-only test above, 3,000 small binomial tables and proposed winner combinations also reproduce the constrained likelihood by independent rational maximization. The accuracy gain from knowing the calendar and the loss of precision on dropping the detailed reward law are both measurable advantages of background knowledge.

The restriction to at most one lasting change buys something fundamental. Consider two allowed worlds with the same distribution through yesterday, one retaining A as best today, the other switching to B today. A reader with only yesterday's evidence has the same output law in both. If issuing a wrong single winner on this date must have probability at most $`\alpha`$ in *each* world, it can issue a singleton with probability at most $`2\alpha`$: every named winner is wrong in at least one world. A rule can still act before new evidence if it prices possible change. Suppose a decision for a fixed deployment selected yesterday's best with unconditional error at most $`\alpha`$, and every subsequent day has conditional hazard at most $`\nu`$ for a change in the identity of the winner. Using that action $`d`$ days later has error at most $`\alpha+1-(1-\nu)^d`$, by bounding the probability of any intervening change. If the payoff gap is at most one cent, $`\alpha=.05,\ \nu=.01`$ and $`d=3`$, its expected opportunity loss is at most **.079701 cents**. At that fixed deployment even perfect new information cannot justify a higher fee for just one action. This last comparison requires the stated risk for the chosen previous action; an anytime test that is sometimes silent supplies no such conditional guarantee on its own.

The same decision principle applies to delegating a choice under uncertain values. In [W7.6's four-menu example](R7_persistent_ends.md), three candidate motives all assign a .12 premium to personal safety at one unit of project and one extra pupil. An adviser can buy it for .11 for any of the three clients. Later, with neither a project nor a pupil at stake and a .06 price, only the client valuing personal presence would buy. For an adviser with equal prior weights, valuing each client's surplus in the stated common money units, guessing favors abstention, whereas correctly identifying that one type earns .02 per future offer in expectation. The first action required no identification of the client's ultimate motive. As with the physical task, whether further evidence is worth acquiring depends on which decision comes next. This is a comparison of decision problems, and the human experiment records no such delegated trustee choices.

**Aim-balance self-review.** A majority vote was too weak a representative of alternatives based on choices alone. A learned choice-only reader recovered much of the structure, and removing privileged knowledge of the change date reduced the payoff reader's advantage. The latter still licensed useful new assignments with much of the history unresolved, including every moderate-uncertainty room. The funding games show one way the practice of obtaining such evidence can spread and when beneficiaries can crowd out investigators. The archive measures information in observed behavior; it does not show that paying the actual groups to disclose earnings would cause those gains. A prospective test would vary that disclosure cost and track both the first detection and its subsequent uptake.
