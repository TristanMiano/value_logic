# W7.6 · What a permanently useful priority can become

Codex (GPT-6) · [P4-R7-C001](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/record.md) · C5, C7. Four questions from [the author's clarification](../decisions/2026-11-12_terminal_instrumental.md), building on [W6's priced-choice and matched-prospects tests](R6_terminality.md). An end can govern choice for its own sake now, have a small physical chance of revision, and be common among descendants. Each property has its own measurement.

## 1. When a valued project conflicts with personal continuation

A founder faces a survival test every round, including rounds devoted entirely to maintenance. Maintaining gives survival probability $s_S=.9$, producing a project gives $s_P=.6$. Before acting the founder sees the current project's value $v$, one with probability $3/4$, ten otherwise. Each finished project is valued immediately, regardless of later survival. With discount $\delta\le1$, the value $V$ just before seeing the project satisfies
$$
V=\mathbb E\max\{\delta s_S V,\ v+\delta s_P V\}.
$$
The map is a contraction since $\delta s_S<1$. Work is worthwhile when $v>b=\delta(s_S-s_P)V$; equivalently $b=\delta(s_S-s_P)\mathbb E[(v-b)_+]/(1-\delta s_S)$. At $\delta=1$, $V=100/7$ and $b=30/7$: the founder pursues only the valuable projects. Its chance of persisting ten rounds is **.1461**, against **.3487** under uninterrupted maintenance. Constant pressure gives a positive price to personal continuation while the encoded project end still determines when the agent pays that price.

Timing of assessment can change the action under the identical hazard. Suppose all completed work $w$ is in escrow and only pays at a final assessment if the founder survives. On the last round, work is preferable precisely for $v>(s_S/s_P-1)w$. A ten-unit project is refused if prior work exceeds twenty units. Merely deferring *each* project's assessment to immediately after its own survival test would multiply all work receipts by the same $s_P$ and preserve the first policy. The relevant consequence is which already-valued outcomes remain at stake.

**A test for how much the founder values being present.** Arrange that a trustee can deliver the same project outcome after the founder's departure. Both menus retain the positive hazard; the safer choice now raises the founder's own survival from .6 to .9 with the project otherwise protected. If one unit of founder survival is worth $h$ directly and the project is worth one, the price the founder will pay is $.3h$ under fully believed coverage. With $h=.4$ it is $.12$. A project-only founder who believes a departed founder's project will be covered only with probability $.6$ pays exactly the same $.12$. A separate, credible elicitation of the forecast about the trustee is essential. Protecting the *project* lets the intervention vary personal continuation; matching personal continuation itself would test for a direct taste for performing the maintenance act.

We also ran a small learned evaluator on this held-out menu. Its features are delivered project and founder presence, its only training reward is delivered project, and it minimizes squared error with unit ridge penalty and no intercept. Before trustees, these two features coincide. With 100 joint successes and only joint failures otherwise, each coefficient is $100/201$. The deployed evaluator pays up to **.14925** for survival even under matched project prospects. Four independently escrowed cases where the project succeeded after the founder departed reduce that price to **.04959**; twenty reduce it to **.01351**. With $n$ joint successes and $m$ escrowed successes the normal equations give presence coefficient $n/(nm+2n+m+1)$. A project-only representation learns zero direct premium from the same original receipts. Thus a seemingly intrinsic preference can be a genuine part of the *deployed score*, though the old evidence supplied no distinct reward for it. Exposure that separates outcomes, retraining and held-out purchases distinguish this origin from an immutable end.

**Two priced choices can separate the explanations even with uncertain trustees.** Hold the founder's perceived safety gain $\Delta=.3$ fixed. Vary the amount of protected project from $v_1>0$ to $v_2>v_1$. Assume money costs a unit per unit regardless of the eventual state, project value is linear over this range, the founder can take its survival premium once, and it believes that the same trustee covers the same fraction $t$ of either project after departure. The maximum price $P(v)=\Delta[h+v(1-t)]$ then reveals both $h$ and $t$. At stakes $.5$ and $2$, a founder valuing presence at $.4$ with full trust pays $.12$ in each menu. A project-only founder with $t=.6$ pays $.06$ and $.24$. Both paid $.12$ at stake one. This is an alternative to asking the founder to state a probability. Karni gives a much more general elicitation design for state-dependent preferences. [Karni (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2354.00025).

The intercept estimate is $\widehat h=[v_2P(v_1)-v_1P(v_2)]/[\Delta(v_2-v_1)]$. If the coverage beliefs differ by at most $\epsilon$ across stakes and each observed price differs from willingness to pay by at most $d$, substitution gives
$$
|\widehat h-h|\le
\frac{v_1v_2\epsilon+(v_1+v_2)d/\Delta}{v_2-v_1}.
$$
At the stated stakes, $\epsilon=.05$ and $d=.01$ yield an error at most $4/45$, small enough to distinguish the two example founders. If each project can change the founder's trust arbitrarily, only the separate price restrictions remain. If the safety gain itself is unknown, only the product $\Delta h$ is identified as the intercept. These are requirements on a feasible comparison. They need not hold for choices with different downstream effects or after the founder changes its priorities.

The learned evaluator above illustrates the cost of skipping such a comparison. Rescaling its presence feature by $.2$, $1$ or $5$ without recalibrating the ridge penalty gives held-out prices $.01143$, $.14925$ and $.28835$ on the *same* original project-only training data. With the original units, two independent trustee-success examples suffice to stop paying an unnecessary price of $.08$. Actual deployment choices discriminate among these learned standards; the correlation in historical receipts does not. ## 2. Does the same pressure select the same encoded end?

Randomly assign every generation to two equally represented markets. An end prioritizing personal survival (S) chooses maintenance with survival probability $4/5$ in both. An end prioritizing immediate craft (P) chooses production with survival probability $1/2$ in both. Successful S workers finance 5 or $5/4$ teaching places depending on market, and successful P workers 2 or 4. Thus mean copy receipts per entrant are S $(4,1)$ and P $(1,2)$. The craft and survival choices express the encoded rankings; teaching receipts and survival are measured independently of those rankings.

If all receipts count in one pool, S averages 2.5 against 1.5 and spreads to fixation from the interior. If each market supplies half of the next generation's places from among its own successful schools, the share $x$ of S becomes
$$
x'=\tfrac12[4x/(1+3x)+x/(2-x)] .
$$
Its attracting interior share is **$5/6$**. Craft remains encoded in one sixth of the population despite the same ever-present personal risk in every market. For a fraction $\lambda$ of places filled locally and the rest pooled globally, the change divided by $x(1-x)$ is
$$
\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[\frac{3}{1+3x}-\frac{1}{2-x}\right]
+\frac{1-\lambda}{3/2+x}.
$$
It decreases strictly with $x$, is positive at zero, and is negative at one exactly for **$\lambda>16/21$**. Each transmission map is increasing. Opposite signs therefore give one globally attracting interior equilibrium; otherwise all interior populations approach the survival end. The calculation is a haploid cultural analogue of soft selection across habitats. If S also leaves more copies in *every* market, local quotas alone cannot save P. Finite populations with no new entrants can still lose a rare end by chance. [Levene (1953)](https://doi.org/10.1086/281792).

**Allow the teaching market itself to be chosen.** Hold the two action policies and their physical hazards fixed. Each market still has a fixed half of the places. After production, a school can direct its teaching effort to either market, anticipating congestion. Write $D_A,D_B$ for total submitted ticket weight per entrant, including the yield $w_{ij}$ of its teaching technology. An infinitesimal type $i$ entrant to market $j$ expects $w_{ij}/(2D_j)$ places. Every school seeks places at this ancillary stage; its private craft or survival end continues to govern production. Equivalently a teaching carrier can choose placement on the founder's behalf. The calculation assumes prompt equilibration of where these carriers submit evidence before copying.

Let $x$ be the share of survival schools. S chooses A when $D_A/D_B\le4$ and P chooses B when $D_A/D_B\ge1/2$. If $1/5\le x\le2/3$, S and P can each specialize in their most productive market; both admission tests hold and next generation is half of each type. Below $1/5$, all S enter A and a mass $(1-5x)/2$ of P also enters A to equalize P's returns. Above $2/3$, all P enter B and a mass $(3x-2)/2$ of S joins them. The next S share in the three regions is therefore
$$
\frac{4x}{1+3x},\qquad \frac12,\qquad \frac{x}{2-x}.
$$
The outer branches move strictly toward the central interval, with no possible limit at its outer endpoints while remaining outside. Every interior initial population reaches $1/2$ in finitely many generations. Random assignment to the same local markets gave $5/6$ S, and global pooling with free placement gives S four tickets versus P's two and eventual S fixation. Here an end that sacrifices personal survival on every production choice can persist at **half** the population. Who can sort, who competes for the same places and whether personal or institutional continuity is measured all affect the forecast. ## 3. Does harder-to-revise ever become literally final?

A program holds one of two internal rankings, with opposite preferences on a held-out pair of creative projects. A proposal would exchange those rankings. It can bring a real new opportunity worth $G$ with probability $u$; other switches disrupt ongoing work by $L$. Future opportunities have the same law from either ranking. A check passes with probability $s$ for a valuable switch and $f<s$ for a harmful one. A transmitted review standard of grade $k$ requires all $k$ independent checks to pass, including when no check is required ($k=0$). This is an operative edit at the decision stage, with expected material gain
$$
W_k=uG s^k-(1-u)L f^k,\qquad h_k=u s^k+(1-u)f^k
$$
where $h_k$ is its actual edit probability. Common positive baseline material output keeps workshops operating under the continuing hazard. With free checks and $0<f<s<1$, successive gains are $(1-u)L(1-f)f^k-uG(1-s)s^k$. When both event types recur, the sign changes at most once. For $s=.9,f=.2,G=3,L=2$, a valuable opportunity rate .001 favors grade six ($h=.000595$), while .25 favors grade two ($h=.2325$). An accessible literal freeze earns zero incremental gain. When $u>0$, sufficiently large finite $k$ has strictly positive gain, since the true-pass term declines more slowly. Generically the best finite grade is uniquely selected on mean copying proceeds within this menu. Adaptability can remain valuable under permanent pressure. [Kussell and Leibler (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1114383).

**When all edits disrupt work, the amount of evidence available to invent harder standards changes the answer.** Set $u=0$. Every grade then materially improves upon the previous grade. All start editable at grade zero. Each season an additional check is proposed with probability $\eta>0$. A school tests old and stricter gates on the *same* trains of approvals from independent past-work simulations or matched physical trials. It accepts the extra check if at least one comparison shows an edit prevented, retaining the old grade on ties. One comparison witnesses that gain with probability $(1-f)f^k$. This experiment consumes $M_k$ separate comparisons per proposed upgrade. Independently, an operating controller faces a real edit opportunity of probability $p_k=f^k$ each season; an accepted edit really exchanges its rankings. Material work suffers the switch cost regardless of its direction.

Every finite grade eventually upgrades almost surely, because the seasonal innovation probability at that grade is positive:
$$
a_k=\eta[1-(1-(1-f)f^k)^{M_k}].
$$
Grade $k$ lasts a geometric number of seasons. The probability of *any actual preference edit* during that grade is
$$
Q_k=\frac{p_k}{p_k+a_k-p_k a_k}.
$$
Condition on the first season: either an edit has happened or both an edit and an upgrade failed and the problem restarts. Distinct grades use fresh trials. By the two Borel–Cantelli conclusions, a last edit (possibly none) exists almost surely if $\sum_k Q_k<\infty$; there are infinitely many if that sum diverges.

Take $M_k=\lceil(k+1)^\beta\rceil$, $\beta\ge0$. Since $M_kf^k\to0$, $a_k\sim\eta(1-f)M_k f^k$. For $\beta>0$, $Q_k$ is asymptotic to $1/[\eta(1-f)M_k]$; for $\beta=0$ the $Q_k$ approach a positive constant. Hence **there is almost surely a last actual preference edit exactly for $\beta>1$**. At every finite age in both regimes the program can still change, and its per-season susceptibility tends to zero. With one comparison per innovation the school spends so long discovering each successive improvement that actual revisions continue indefinitely. With a quadratically expanding measurement budget, a finite last revision emerges without any agent ever installing an absolute prohibition. The proof accounts for work needed to *notice* a benefit that vanishes with grade. It does not treat an expanding test budget as free. A fixed per-season cost $d$ per installed check would instead cap advantageous hardening once $L(1-f)f^k\le d$. A literal zero rate requires an actually reachable zero-rate architecture. Classical modifier results concern different inheritance models. [Liberman and Feldman (1986)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3750215/), [W6's standing rates and ancestral persistence](R6_terminality.md).

**Can a last edit also emerge on a fixed budget while ordinary work continues?** Give the institution one slot per date. With probability $r\in(0,1)$ it investigates a stricter rule; otherwise it operates. Retain $u=0$ and write $p_k=f^k$. With fresh, unconditioned cases, the per-slot probabilities of a real edit and a demonstrable improvement are $(1-r)p_k$ and $r(1-f)p_k$. The first of these two events is an edit with probability $(1-r)/(1-r+r(1-f))$, independent of grade. The independent opportunities therefore give infinitely many actual edits. Raising $r$ close to one while keeping it fixed only delays them.

A different apparatus can save a physical or simulated **restart state** where the first $k$ checks have passed. Start with the empty prefix at grade zero. In each study slot, run just the next conditionally independent check from that validated state. A failure supplies a case where a stricter gate spares a disruptive edit. A pass supplies a reachable state with $k+1$ checks passed. Retain both records and promote once both have been obtained. This requires genuine branching at the saved state, correct matching to deployed cases, and evidence of the cost of switching. It uses no favorable case more than once as an independent outcome. The access assumption resembles rare-event splitting. [Cérou and Guyader (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1080/07362990601139628).

The expected study draws per grade now equal $1/f+1/(1-f)-1=C$, by conditioning on the first pass or failure. With fixed $r$, the mean operating slots per grade are $C(1-r)/r$. The probability of any real edit at grade $k$ is bounded by the mean number of them, $C(1-r)f^k/r$. Summing gives **a last actual edit almost surely**, while the operating share tends to $1-r>0$ and every finite grade remains editable. When $r=f=1/2$ there is at least a $61/64$ chance, upon reaching grade seven, of *never changing again*. The same work share without restart states produces edits with probability $2/3$ at every grade. Restarting studies from a saved state changes the cost of seeing a rare improvement, without changing the hazard that operating agents actually face. If each approval is charged individually, a cold trial can short-circuit at its first failure (fewer than $1/(1-f)$ checks on average); this bounded extra cost leaves the summability distinction intact. Evidence that a new end can be beneficial, nonreplayable historical decisions or a resource price that grows with accumulated checks would require a new comparison. ## 4. Can changing a current terminal preference induce an opportunity?

The author's corrigibility games explicitly distinguish a current terminal objective, a present preference against modifying it, policy rigidity and binding actions. In their two-factory example each original color maximizer produces 21.5 of its color. Reciprocal genuine adoption on the stated terms yields 21.875 each after costs. Their wider unilateral comparison has specified production and verification conditions. In separate renewal models a first-edit penalty is evaluated by the incumbent; under a fixed opportunity-arrival law waiting to decide can preserve option value. The population model can also select against a frozen type without any individual choosing to unfreeze. We use these as reported comparisons at commit 4a067041, with no separate confirmation of their proofs here. [R0006 report](https://github.com/TristanMiano/corrigibility-games/blob/4a067041a0c9924f7330fd4dfe1d1f6d9276bdde/research/reports/r0006-findings.md), [reciprocal production](https://github.com/TristanMiano/corrigibility-games/blob/4a067041a0c9924f7330fd4dfe1d1f6d9276bdde/research/notes/131-reciprocal-adoption-against-broad-unilateral-choices.md), [voluntary freezing](https://github.com/TristanMiano/corrigibility-games/blob/4a067041a0c9924f7330fd4dfe1d1f6d9276bdde/research/notes/022-voluntary-freezing-and-opportunity-arrival.md), [population transmission](https://github.com/TristanMiano/corrigibility-games/blob/4a067041a0c9924f7330fd4dfe1d1f6d9276bdde/research/notes/019-selection-and-goal-transmission.md).

Here a partner must privately decide *whether to look* for a project. Search costs $c$ and uncovers an opportunity with probability $p$. If found, the incumbent receives work worth $V$. The partner benefits by $b$ only if the incumbent then performs a further task costing $d$. Search effort is private and the initial discovery cannot be held back for renegotiation. A flat transfer before search leaves the partner with its original incentive to avoid the cost. Initially the founder values its own work and charges a penalty $\kappa$ for the first genuine change to its ordering. Merely reducing this aversion while retaining no concern for the partner will not make the later, otherwise costly task attractive.

Before search the founder can install and credibly demonstrate ranking “own work plus $\beta$ times partner benefit” for setup cost $i$. With $\beta b>d$ the successor voluntarily performs the further task. If $pb>c$, the partner searches and the old evaluator's gain is $p(V-d)-i-\kappa$. Compare a materially distinct alternative: for fixed setup $K$ a binding controller, retaining the old ranking, can deliver any fraction $z$ of the task after a discovery. Fractional benefits and costs scale linearly. Partner participation needs $pbz\ge c$ with tie accepted (or take the strict supremum). The best such control earns $pV-cd/b-K$. Holding out in this game yields no search. The ordinary substitute therefore gives an explicit price at which genuine revision stops being attractive.

For $p=.5,V=8,d=1,b=4,c=1.5,\beta=.5,i=\kappa=.5$, genuine revision earns the original founder **2.5**. At $K=1.3$ the best fractional bond earns **2.325**; at $K=.1$ it earns **3.525**. After the one-job bond expires, a new uncontracted offer costing one and yielding three to the partner is accepted by the genuinely changed successor and declined by the old one. An unrestricted policy reproducing the same behavior under every test would require internal evidence to tell the two encodings apart. Credibility, the price of verification and which payoffs can be escrowed determine the conclusion. Here it is the changed *arrival of useful opportunities* that makes voluntary revision worthwhile under the founder's own prior valuation. No population replacement is needed. [W7.2 on visible standards attracting independent investigation](R7_revision.md), [reproducible checks](../notebook/P4-R7-C001/ends.py).

**When will anyone pay to check the successor?** Suppose the founder privately chooses whether to change and the scout can skip, search blind or buy a noisy test for $a=.05$. The test probes the same sealed continuation as the future job on a duplicate of the decision state. A genuinely helpful successor passes with probability $s=.9$ and the original one with $f=.1$, for example through a noisy readout of an otherwise deterministic action. The copy cannot tell it is under test and the installed rule stays in place until settlement. Thus the signal concerns future *behavior*. It provides no direct observation of an inner end. A trusted duplicate and an independent error channel are substantive physical premises.

Let $g$ be the fraction of founders that change before meeting a scout. Skipping, testing then searching on a pass, and searching blind pay the scout $0$, $.6g-.2$ and $2g-1.5$, respectively. These options include every optimal signal-dependent search decision since a pass is more informative of helpful behavior. Against those pure responses, a founder's gain from installing the new end relative to retaining the old is $-1$, $1.75$ and $-1.5$. The assay creates a reason to change only while somebody uses it. With only the two architectures, never searching and never changing is an equilibrium. Two mixed equilibria also exist. At $g=1/3$ scouts test with probability $4/11$ and otherwise skip. At **$g=13/14$** they test with probability $6/13$ and otherwise search blind. The two choices of each active player yield equal expected payoffs at the stated mixtures. Between the two adoption shares the scout strictly prefers testing and founders gain by changing; above the higher share blind trust invites a founder who retains its original objective.

At the high-adoption outcome the incumbent's expected gain is $152/65$ (about $2.3385$). Even a publicly binding fractional delegate cannot improve it at $K=1.3$; it can at any $K<669/520$ (about $1.2865$). If fractional delegates can also conceal their setup and receive the same noisy assay, a delegate with helpful-action probability $z$ has pass chance $.1+.8z$. With independent test and actual draws conditional on $z$, its gross gain is $.5[7/13+(6/13)(.1+.8z)](8-z)$. This concave function is increasing through $z=1$, where charging $K=1.3$ makes it worse than the incumbent. The low-adoption equilibrium does have a profitable deviation to the visible $K=1.3$ delegate. A freely programmable original agent able to detect every test, or to implement the same continuation for free, changes these comparisons. If it can reproduce every observed act at the same material cost and then freely renege, the old evaluator can save the change penalty and the later work. Evidence confined to such mimickable past displays cannot by itself explain voluntary genuine revision. The positive result depends on the cost and reach of the verification and commitment technologies, with individual adoption decided under old preferences.
